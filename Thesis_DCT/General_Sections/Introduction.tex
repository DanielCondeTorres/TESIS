\subsection{The Lipid Membrane: A Selective Biological Landscape}

To understand how Antimicrobial Peptides (AMPs) operate, we must first examine the stage upon which these interactions unfold: the lipid membrane. Rather than viewing lipids merely through the lens of chemical solubility, it is more productive to define them as the fundamental architectural units of biological boundaries. Among these, phospholipids are the primary constituents and the most relevant to the mechanical and structural integrity of the cell\cite{van2008membrane}. Structurally, a phospholipid is defined by a central alcohol backbone that serves as a bridge, anchoring a polar, phosphate-rich head group to a pair of nonpolar, hydrophobic chains (\textbf{Figure \ref{fig:Lipids}}). This inherent amphipathic nature is what drives their spontaneous self-assembly into the bilayer structures that define all cellular interfaces\cite{self-assembly}. The hydrophobic tails aggregate in the interior to minimize contact with water, while the hydrophilic heads orient toward the aqueous environment, forming a stable, semi-permeable barrier\cite{self-assembly}. The specific chemical identity of the head group (e.g., choline, ethanolamine, serine) dictates the net charge of the surface, a crucial factor in determining the electrostatic interactions that govern the recruitment and insertion of molecules like AMPs\cite{cationic-anionic-interaction}.

\subsubsection{Beyond Protein Targets: The Membrane as a Vulnerability}

Traditional pharmacology has historically relied on antibiotics that bind to specific protein receptors or inhibit metabolic pathways \cite{Lohner2009}. While this high specificity can be effective, it also represents a strategic limitation, as microorganisms can rapidly bypass these effects through point mutations or adaptive metabolic shifts, leading to the emergence of resistance. Consequently, there is a critical need for antimicrobial agents that target more fundamental and less mutable molecular features \cite{Ahmed2024}.

The lipid composition of cellular membranes represents a promising target in this context, as it plays a central role in the physiology and pathology of diverse cell types, ranging from bacteria to mammalian cells, including cancer cells. The differences in lipidic composition open the possibility of exploiting the lipidic composition as a molecular target \cite{Sani2015}. In healthy mammalian cells, the cytoplasmic membrane exhibits asymmetry\cite{Ikeda2006}: the outer leaflet is enriched in phosphatidylcholine (PC), cholesterol, and sphingomyelin, while the inner leaflet primarily contains phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) and phosphatidylserine (PS)\cite{bilayer_asimetry_1}. This asymmetry generates a neutral external surface and a negatively charged internal surface, facilitating electrostatic interactions with cytoplasmic proteins and contributing to membrane stability and fluidity, a property further modulated by cholesterol\cite{bilayer_asimetry_2,bilayer_asimetry_3}.

Bacterial membranes, in contrast, display markedly different lipid compositions that vary across species. For example, in Gram-negative bacteria such as \textit{Escherichia coli}, the cytoplasmic membrane is predominantly composed of PE ($\approx$75\%), PG ($\approx$20\%), and a smaller fraction of cardiolipin\cite{bacterial_bilayer_1,bacterial_bilayer_2,Geiger2013,Vance2015}, contrasting sharply with mammalian membranes. These differences define the bilayer structure and influence essential processes such as molecular transport and signaling\cite{bacterial_bilayer_3}. Furthermore, the relative proportions of bacterial membrane lipids can change during the cell cycle, enabling adaptation to environmental stressors\cite{bacteria_bilayer_4}.

Cancer cells also exhibit distinctive alterations in membrane lipid composition that facilitate tumor progression and immune evasion. A key feature is the abnormal external exposure of PS, which in healthy cells signals macrophages to engulf apoptotic cells\cite{cancer_bilayer_1}. In cancer cells, however, this external PS acts as a protective mechanism by inhibiting immune responses\cite{cancer_bilayer_2,cancer_bilayer_3}. Reduced cholesterol levels in cancer membranes further increase fluidity, enhancing cellular motility and promoting metastasis\cite{cancer_bilayer_4}.



These observations highlight that the lipid composition of membranes is not only a fundamental determinant of cell function but also a promising target for therapeutic intervention. By focusing on membrane lipids, it may be possible to develop selective antimicrobial or anticancer strategies that act on structural features less susceptible to rapid resistance, offering an advantage over conventional drugs that target specific proteins or metabolic pathways\cite{membrane_therapy}. This shift away from volatile protein receptors is precisely where contemporary lipidomics provides its greatest value, unveiling the cell membrane not as a static perimeter, but as a complex, tightly regulated system \cite{lipidomics_1,lipidomics_2}. While these cellular boundaries diverge from healthy eukaryotic tissues across multiple morphological and organizational levels \cite{bacterial_bilayer_1,bacterial_bilayer_2, cancer_bilayer_1,cancer_bilayer_2,cancer_bilayer_3,cancer_bilayer_4}, it is this precise variation in lipid composition that offers a uniquely tractable basis for engineering highly selective antimicrobial and anticancer strategies \cite{lipidomics_4}.

In the search for new antimicrobial strategies, nature provides an important source of inspiration. As part of their innate immune systems, many animals and plants produce antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) across a variety of tissues and cell types \cite{Zasloff2002}. These peptides have attracted particular interest due to their ability to exploit fundamental physicochemical differences between bacterial and eukaryotic membranes, and are often hypothesized to interact preferentially with membrane compositions enriched in negatively charged lipids\cite{Midura-Nowaczek2014}.

\section{The Global Crisis of Antimicrobial Resistance}

The exponential rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses one of the most critical public health and economic threats of the 21st century \cite{salam2023antimicrobial}. With traditional antibiotics losing their efficacy due to the rapid mutation and adaptation of pathogens, the discovery and optimization of novel therapeutic agents is urgently required \cite{macnair2024alternative}. Indeed, it is estimated that by 2050, more than 10 million deaths annually will be associated with AMR if current trends continue \cite{Tang2023}.

To address this impending clinical crisis, research has pivoted toward ancient host-defense mechanisms, among which Antimicrobial Peptides (AMPs) have garnered immense attention. AMPs are ancient, evolutionarily conserved effector molecules of the innate immune system found across all domains of life, from bacteria and fungi to plants and mammals \cite{Hancock2006, Kumar2018, fermentation10110540, microorganisms13112574}. Unlike conventional small-molecule antibiotics, which typically target highly specific enzymatic pathways, AMPs employ rapid, physically driven, and multi-target mechanisms—primarily driven by biophysical membrane disruption—that significantly diminish the likelihood of bacteria developing mutational resistance \cite{microorganisms13112574, 10.3389/fmicb.2020.582779}.

Structurally, these peptides represent a highly diverse class of small biomolecules, typically consisting of 10 to 60 amino acids. While the vast majority are inherently cationic to facilitate electrostatic recruitment toward the anionic surfaces of microbial envelopes, several anionic AMPs have also been reported, with up to 187 entries listed in the Antimicrobial Peptide Database (APD3) as of December 2024 \cite{Wang2016}. This extensive structural and functional diversity makes a unified classification challenging; nevertheless, they are broadly categorized based on their biological source, spectrum of activity, conformation, or amino acid-rich motifs (Figure \ref{fig:AMPClass}) \cite{Huan2020}.

Their biological activity is commonly linked to their cationic nature, which allows electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged membranes of invading pathogens rather than with the neutrally charged membranes of eukaryotic cells \cite{Zhang2021}. Additionally, hydrophobicity---defined as the percentage of hydrophobic residues---is another key feature, as it influences the ability of AMPs to partition into membrane lipid bilayers \cite{Yeaman2003}. AMPs usually present a hydrophobicity of around 50\%, which, together with their cationicity, typically leads to amphipathic structures. In many cases, AMPs are largely unstructured in aqueous solution but undergo a conformational transition to $\alpha$-helical structures upon interaction with lipid membranes, a process that enhances their amphipathic character and membrane-disruptive potential \cite{Li2021}. While some AMPs display their antimicrobial activity by means of non-lytic mechanisms \cite{Scocchi2016}, the most common modes of action involve membranolytic effects driven by their amphiphatic nature (Figure \ref{fig:AMPMOA}). In a first stage, electrostatic interactions with negatively charged components of the membranes promote the accumulation of AMPs. Next, the membrane's non-polar core interacts with the peptides' hydrophobic residues, facilitating their insertion, disturbing membrane structure, and ultimately leading to cell death\cite{Mookherjee2020,BuiThiPhuong2024}.

As of its latest update in December 2024  [Retrieved June 2026], the Data Repository of Antimicrobial Peptides (DRAMP)\cite{Ma2025} lists 35918 entries under the query \textir{antimicrobial}. Nevertheless, only a few AMPs have reached clinical trials, and over 100 peptide-based drugs have been approved worldwide\cite{Rossino2023,Pereira2024}. A major limitation of AMPs is their metabolic instability, as they are rapidly degraded by proteolytic enzymes\cite{Dijksteel2021}. They also tend to exhibit toxicity toward host cells, which limits their therapeutic application\cite{Lyu2023}. In addition, they tend to have short half-lives and are quickly cleared from the bloodstream, which results in poor bioavailability. To overcome these limitations, various strategies have been investigated, such us incorporating non-natural amino acids and peptide cyclization to improve stability and minimize degradation\cite{Carmona2013,Lucana2021}.

In this context, this thesis addresses these computational and biophysical bottlenecks by focusing on unlocking the specificity of antimicrobial peptide interactions for membrane-targeted therapies. Instead of relying on empirical chemical modifications, this work establishes a foundational framework aimed at a precise, molecular-level understanding of peptide-lipid recognition. By dissecting the fundamental forces that govern how these biomolecules distinguish between diverse cellular membranes, we provide the mechanistic groundwork needed to exploit lipid composition as a highly selective therapeutic target.

Building upon this conceptual milestone, the subsequent phases of this research transition into advanced computational methodologies to model and engineer these interactions from first principles. Specifically, we explore the frontier of structural biology by performing classical simulations on quantum computers to investigate interface-driven peptide folding on simulated membrane surfaces, capturing the highly dynamic conformational changes that occur precisely at the lipid-water boundary. Ultimately, the insights gained from these quantum-simulated systems are translated into practical blueprints for the environment-conditioned design of $\alpha$-helical peptides, effectively establishing a predictive framework for the targeted design of membrane-specific peptides.
 


\section{Physicochemical Properties and Structural Determinants}
The biological functionality of an AMP is intrinsically linked to its primary amino acid sequence and resulting three-dimensional topology. Most native AMPs are short oligomers, typically comprising 10 to 60 amino acid residues. Their activity is governed by two main factors:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cationicity:} Most AMPs possess a net positive charge (typically ranging from +2 to +9), primarily due to the presence of Lysine (Lys, K) and Arginine (Arg, R) residues. This favors electrostatic interaction with the negatively charged membranes of pathogens.
    \item \textbf{Amphipathicity:} AMPs exhibit a balance between hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions. This dual nature is essential for their insertion into the non-polar lipid core of biological membranes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Structural Classification}
Based on the secondary structure they acquire when interacting with their targets, AMPs are generally classified into four archetypes:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{$\alpha$-helical peptides:} These are the most abundant in nature (e.g., magainins, LL-37). They often remain in a random coil state in solution but undergo a conformational change to an $\alpha$-helix upon adsorption into the lipid bilayer.
    \item \textbf{$\beta$-sheet peptides:} These structures are stabilized by intramolecular disulfide bridges (Cys-Cys bonds), providing high resistance to proteolytic degradation. Examples include human defensins.
    \item \textbf{Loop/Cyclic peptides:} These form structures via head-to-tail cyclization or single disulfide bonds (e.g., bactenecins).
    \item \textbf{Extended peptides:} These lack traditional secondary folding and are often rich in specific residues like Proline or Tryptophan (e.g., indolicidin).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Mechanisms of Membrane Disruption}
The therapeutic action of membrane-targeting AMPs is achieved through the physical disruption of the cell membrane. This process occurs in two primary stages: initial electrostatic recruitment followed by hydrophobic partitioning. Once a critical concentration of peptides is adsorbed, the membrane structure is compromised through several proposed models:

\subsection{Pore Formation Models}
Depending on the peptide's characteristics, two main pore models are described:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Barrel-stave model:} Peptides insert perpendicularly into the membrane, aggregating such that their hydrophobic faces interact with lipid tails while their hydrophilic faces form a central aqueous channel.
    \item \textbf{Toroidal-pore model:} Peptides induce a local curvature in the membrane, causing the lipid headgroups to tilt and line the pore along with the peptides.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Non-Pore Models}
Alternatively, some peptides act via the \textbf{Carpet model}, where they accumulate parallel to the membrane surface. Upon reaching a threshold, they act like detergents, leading to the micellization and fragmentation of the bilayer without forming discrete pores.



\section{Limitations of Experimental Techniques}
The study of Antimicrobial Peptides (AMPs) and their interactions with lipid membranes is highly complex, typically requiring simplified experimental models such as surfactant micelles, binary lipid mixtures, or helix-stabilizing co-solvents like TFE \cite{paper_paula, experiments_1,experiments_2,experiments_3}. While these controlled environments are easier to manipulate than real pathogens, their vast diversity complicates cross-literature comparisons. Because the secondary structure of membrane-targeting AMPs—especially those with $\alpha$-helical content—is highly sensitive to its medium \cite{experiment_4,experiment_5}, it is challenging to directly extrapolate these experimental findings to real physiological conditions without ensuring identical testing parameters.
To characterize these interactions, researchers rely heavily on a combination of structural and kinetic techniques, primarily Circular Dichroism (CD), Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR), and Fluorescence spectroscopy\cite{general_1,cd_1,cd_2,nmr_1,nmr_2,fluo_1,afm_1}. CD estimates secondary structure and membrane distribution \cite{cd_1,cd_2} by measuring the differential absorption of circularly polarized light\cite{cd_4}, though it only provides an ensemble average rather than individual molecular details\cite{cd_3}. NMR complements this by generating 3-dimensional average structures using magnetic spin alignment\cite{nmr_1,nmr_2}. Meanwhile, fluorescence assays, utilizing intrinsic amino acids like Tryptophan or covalently attached tags \cite{fluo_1}, help quantify membrane binding and vesicle leakage\cite{fluo_3,fluo_4}, although artificial fluorophores must be used cautiously to avoid altering the peptide's inherent physicochemical properties\cite{fluo_2}.
Finally, morphological and thermodynamic behaviors are assessed using Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) and calorimetry. AFM visualizes topological defects and pore formation mechanisms\cite{afm_1,afm_2}, but it is limited by the requirement of unnaturally flat supported bilayers that fail to replicate the curvature of vesicles in solution\cite{afm_6}. Calorimetry measures enthalpy changes to determine binding affinity and selectivity\cite{afm_4,afm_5}. Despite the necessity of combining these diverse techniques to build a comprehensive picture, a critical overarching limitation remains: none of these conventional experimental methods can independently provide time-resolved, atomic-level insights into the dynamic processes of AMP-membrane interactions\cite{afm_1,afm_2,cd_3}.
\section{Simulation Approaches: Molecular Dynamics (MD)}
Unlike experimental techniques, the simulation of systems involving peptides and lipid bilayers can provide atomistic, time-resolved trajectories of all particles in the system. This allows the study of peptide adsorption and folding at a level of detail beyond what is achieved experimentally.

\subsection{Classical Molecular Dynamics}
Classical MD computes the motion of particles by integrating Newton’s equations of motion. The accuracy of these simulations depends on the \textbf{Force Field} (FF), which provides the parameters for bonded and non-bonded interactions. The choice of FF is critical; if the selected FF does not capture the relevant interactions (e.g., specific protein-lipid affinities), the resulting trajectories will not represent the physical system.

\subsection{Solvent and Membrane Representation}
Early simulations used \textbf{implicit solvent} models to save computational costs, representing water as a continuous medium with a specific dielectric constant. However, this fails to capture specific hydrogen bonding between the peptide and water molecules. Modern MD favors \textbf{explicit solvent} and \textbf{explicit membranes}, where every lipid and water molecule is represented. While more accurate, this significantly increases the system size and computational demand.

\section{Sampling Challenges and Enhanced Methods}
The main limitation of atomistic MD is the timescale. Slow phenomena such as spontaneous peptide folding or pore formation often occur on microsecond to millisecond scales, which are difficult to reach with standard MD. To overcome this, several \textbf{Enhanced Sampling} techniques are employed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Replica-Exchange MD (REMD):} Running multiple simulations at different temperatures to cross energy barriers.
    \item \textbf{Metadynamics:} Adding a history-dependent biasing potential to discourage the system from staying in local energy minima.
    \item \textbf{Umbrella Sampling:} Constraining the system along a specific reaction coordinate (e.g., the distance between the peptide and the membrane center).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Coarse-Grained (CG) Simulations}
A Coarse-Grained representation improves sampling by reducing the degrees of freedom. In CG models, such as the \textbf{Martini force field}, groups of approximately four heavy atoms are mapped into a single interaction site (bead). 
\begin{equation}
    F_i = m_i \frac{d^2r_i}{dt^2}
\end{equation}
This smoothing of the free energy surface allows for simulations that are orders of magnitude longer. However, the loss of resolution—such as the absence of explicit hydrogen atoms—means that phenomena like precise H-bonding and secondary structure transitions cannot be fully described without additional constraints like elastic networks.



\section{Pharmacological Barriers}
Despite their potential, as of early 2026, only a few AMPs (e.g., daptomycin, polymyxins, and gramicidin D) are in clinical use. The transition from lab to clinic is hindered by:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Proteolytic Instability:} Rapid degradation by proteases in the blood.
    \item \textbf{Cytotoxicity:} High concentrations can lead to hemolysis (destruction of red blood cells).
    \item \textbf{Production Costs:} Large-scale chemical synthesis of long peptides remains expensive.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Advanced Design Strategies}
Modern medicinal chemistry utilizes several strategies to "drug-optimize" AMPs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Peptide Stapling:} Introducing a chemical "bridge" (tether) between residues (e.g., positions $i$ and $i+7$) to lock the peptide into an $\alpha$-helical shape, increasing stability and potency.
    \item \textbf{D-Amino Acids:} Using the mirror-image isomers of amino acids, which proteases cannot recognize, thus extending the peptide's half-life.
    \item \textbf{Lipidation:} Attaching fatty acid chains to enhance membrane anchor properties.
    \item \textbf{Peptidomimetics:} Creating synthetic molecules (like Brilacidin) that mimic the properties of AMPs but are not composed of standard peptide bonds.
\end{itemize}

Antimicrobial peptides represent a frontier in the fight against AMR. While experimental techniques provide the foundation for our understanding, Molecular Dynamics simulations bridge the gap by offering a molecular "movie" of the disruption process. The future of AMP-based therapy lies in the synergy between rational bioengineering and high-performance computational modeling to create stable, selective, and cost-effective agents for clinical use.











































\section{Computational Methods in Chemistry}

As the performance of computational methods is tightly linked to the available computational power, advances in supercomputing and simulation techniques are pushing the boundaries of molecular simulations of biological systems. In parallel, the breakthrough of Artificial Intelligence (AI) is revolutionizing science, by enabling the analysis of huge databases to make accurate predictions. In the field of molecular biophysics and chemistry, MD simulations and ML approaches are increasingly recognized as complementary tools. While ML models have already demonstrated the ability to predict properties such as CP membrane permeability directly from experimental data \cite{Cao2024,Yu2024}, MD simulations provide a unique advantage by generating physically consistent, time-resolved trajectories that describe molecular structure, dynamics, and interactions under well-defined conditions. This makes MD-derived data particularly suitable for training and validating ML models, especially in systems where experimental characterization is challenging. This synergy between Molecular Dynamics and Artificial Intelligence is the main foundation of this doctoral thesis. By combining physics-based modeling with data-driven approaches, this work aims to bridge molecular-level mechanisms and predictive capabilities, contributing to emerging paradigms in computational chemistry.

\subsection{Molecular Dynamics}

MD is a simulation technique used to study the evolution over time of molecular systems by explicitly modeling the motion of atoms or interaction sites through the numerical integration of Newton's equations of motion. At each time step, the forces acting on the particles are computed using force fields, a set of equations and parameters that describe different inter and intramolecular interactions \cite{Ciccotti2022}.

The first MD simulation dates back to 1957, when Wainwright and Alder simulated systems of hard spheres using a discontinuous potential \cite{Alder1957}. The next step was taken by Rahman in 1964, who simulated liquid argon using a Lennard-Jones pairwise additive potential to account for the interactions between argon atoms. During an era in which computers were scarce and limited in speed and memory, advances in algorithms were essential. In 1967, for example, Verlet proposed a stable numerical integration scheme for Newton's equations, an approach that is still widely used today \cite{Verlet1967}. Later on, in 1971, Rahman and Stillinger published for the first time an MD study on a model of liquid water \cite{Rahman1971}, demonstrating that its structure is formed by a dynamic network of hydrogen bonds. Although today this may seem like routine work, at the time it led the scientific community to believe that if it was possible to simulate water, the medium of the chemistry of life, why not simulate biomolecules themselves? The breakthrough came in 1977, when a group led by Karplus published the first MD simulation of a simple protein \cite{McCammon1977}.

During the early 1980s significant progress was made in developing algorithms to sample conditions relevant to experiments. Standard MD simulations operate in the microcanonical (constant energy) ensemble, since Newton's equations of motion conserve the total energy. Andersen was the first to propose a method for achieving constant-pressure MD simulations by extending the dynamical variables of the system to include the volume \cite{Andersen1980}. He was later followed by Parrinello and Rahman, who extended the scheme to include shape fluctuations as well \cite{Parrinello1980,Parrinello1981}. Building on Andersen's work, Nosé introduced a new dynamical variable, coupling the kinetic energy of the atoms to the external temperature, allowing MD to sample the canonical ensemble \cite{Nose1984}. Hoover later refined this approach leading to what is known as Nosé-Hoover thermostat \cite{Hoover1985}, widely used in MD simulations today.

Since then, both the size of the simulated systems and the simulation times have grown exponentially, making it common to find studies in the literature on proteins, DNA/RNA complexes or self-assembly processes such as micelle formation \cite{Karplus2002,Sponer2018,Morrow2013}. More broadly, the impact of molecular simulation approaches on chemical research was formally recognized with the award of the 2013 Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Martin Karplus, Arieh Warshel and Michael Levitt for "the development of multiscale models for complex chemical systems".
%%%
Within this broader landscape, MD simulations have also been applied to the study of cyclic peptides (CPs) and self-assembled cyclic peptide nanotubes (SCPNs), providing molecular-level insight into their conformational behavior, self-assembly mechanisms, and interactions with lipid membranes. Atomistic MD studies offer a level of structural and dynamical detail difficult to access experimentally, helping to elucidate how peptide sequence, chirality, and amphipathicity influence supramolecular organization. Despite these capabilities, previous research in our group has focused specifically on embedded nanotubes, investigating their stability and implications for membrane structure \cite{GF2016,Calvelo2019,Xandre2021,Conde2022}. A shared feature of the cited works was the use of pre-assembled nanotubes that were manually inserted into lipid bilayers, a necessity driven by the high computational cost of the application of atomistic MD to CP and SCPN systems. Key processes such as peptide self-assembly, membrane insertion, and supramolecular rearrangement typically occur on microsecond to millisecond timescales and involve large peptide–membrane assemblies, placing them beyond the practical reach of routine atomistic simulations.

To overcome these limitations, coarse-grained (CG) Molecular Dynamics approaches have been increasingly adopted, as they enable the simulation of larger systems over longer timescales. CG models have been successfully applied to study the interactions of various CP sequences with lipid bilayers, starting either from a pre-assembled nanotube in solution or from randomly positioned units that self-assemble \cite{Claro2020,Claro2021}. However, while CG models are well suited to capture collective phenomena such as aggregation and membrane remodeling, their reduced resolution often compromises the accurate description of direction-specific interactions, including hydrogen-bond networks, backbone geometry, and chirality-dependent stacking. These features are particularly critical for CPs and SCPNs, whose self-assembly is governed by highly directional hydrogen bonding and precise geometric constraints. As a result, existing CG force fields may fail to faithfully reproduce key aspects of cyclic peptide self-assembly and membrane interactions, despite their favorable computational efficiency.

This gap between atomistic accuracy and CG scalability highlights the need for computational strategies that preserve essential structural features of cyclic peptides while enabling systematic, large-scale simulations and data-driven analysis.

Looking forward, MD continue to benefit from advances in high-performance computing, including the deployment of exascale architectures, which enable increasingly large and long simulations of complex molecular systems. In parallel, the breakthrough of AI, and in particular Machine Learning (ML), is profoundly revolutionizing the field. Beyond their application as classification or prediction tools, ML-based approaches are increasingly integrated into the core of theoretical chemistry through the development of ML potentials, which offer a promising route to accelerate MD while preserving atomic accuracy.


















\subsection{Quantum Computing}\label{QC_intro}

A fundamental challenge in the study of antimicrobial peptides arises from the enormous size of both sequence and conformational spaces. For a peptide of length \(L\), the number of possible amino acid sequences scales exponentially as \(20^L\), making exhaustive exploration infeasible even for relatively short peptides. In addition to sequence diversity, each peptide can adopt a vast number of three-dimensional conformations, which are strongly influenced by the surrounding environment, particularly in heterogeneous systems such as lipid membranes.

This complexity is closely related to the Levinthal paradox, which highlights the apparent contradiction between the astronomical number of possible conformations and the relatively fast folding times observed in nature. This paradox suggests that peptide folding is guided by structured energy landscapes rather than random exploration, yet accurately characterizing these landscapes remains a major computational challenge.

From a computational perspective, the protein folding problem has been shown to be NP-hard under several simplified models, implying that no efficient classical algorithm is known to solve it in the general case. This intrinsic computational complexity further limits the applicability of classical methods when attempting to explore both sequence and conformational spaces in a systematic manner.

Recent advances in machine learning approaches, most notably AlphaFold, have achieved remarkable success in predicting static protein structures from amino acid sequences. However, such methods primarily provide high-confidence approximations of equilibrium conformations and do not explicitly model the underlying physical folding pathways, energetic landscapes, or environment-dependent effects such as peptide–lipid interactions. In particular, systems involving membrane interfaces and highly dynamic conformational transitions remain challenging for purely data-driven structural prediction approaches.

Classical approaches, such as molecular dynamics simulations, are inherently constrained by accessible timescales and sampling efficiency, particularly in the presence of rare events and complex energy barriers. As a result, a comprehensive exploration of peptide–membrane interactions remains computationally prohibitive.

These limitations motivate the exploration of alternative computational paradigms capable of addressing the exponential and combinatorial complexity of biomolecular systems. In this context, quantum computing offers a fundamentally different framework, in which the principles of superposition and entanglement enable the representation and manipulation of high-dimensional quantum states more naturally. Although current quantum hardware remains limited, these approaches provide a promising avenue for tackling the intrinsic complexity of peptide folding and sequence exploration problems.



The emergence of Quantum Computing represents a paradigm shift in our ability to process information, rooted in the fundamental principles of quantum mechanics. The field's conceptual origin is often traced back to Richard Feynman's 1982 observation that simulating nature—which is inherently quantum mechanical—requires a machine that operates on the same principles \cite{feynman1982simulating}. As detailed in the seminal work of Nielsen and Chuang \cite{nielsen2010quantum}, quantum computation transcends the limits of the classical Turing machine by utilizing qubits, which can exist in superpositions and entangled states. This allows for the exploration of a Hilbert space that grows exponentially with the number of qubits, offering a route to solve computational problems that are practically intractable for even the most powerful classical supercomputers.

The theoretical promise of quantum advantage was first demonstrated through algorithms such as Shor's for factoring integers and Grover's for database searching. However, the current state of technology, known as the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era \cite{preskill2018quantum}, is characterized by devices with limited qubit counts and high error rates. Despite these limitations, the development of hybrid quantum-classical algorithms has opened new avenues for practical applications. These algorithms, such as the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) \cite{peruzzo2014variational} and the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) \cite{farhi2014quantum}, distribute the computational load between a quantum processor and a classical optimizer, making them remarkably robust against the noise inherent to current hardware.

In the realm of chemistry and biophysics, quantum computing is poised to revolutionize how we understand molecular systems. While classical methods struggle with the electronic correlation energy of large molecules, quantum processors can naturally represent the multi-qubit states of atomic orbitals. Pioneering works have shown that VQE can accurately simulate the potential energy surfaces of small molecules, providing a glimpse into a future where "exact" chemical simulations are routine \cite{mcclean2016theory}. Beyond energy estimation, the application of quantum optimization to protein folding and sequence design represents one of the most exciting frontiers in the field. By mapping conformational landscapes and amino acid interactions onto a qubit space, quantum algorithms can navigate the vast discrete search spaces of molecular design more efficiently than classical heuristic searches \cite{robert2021resource}.

The integration of quantum computing into the existing biophysical toolbox offers a complementary approach to traditional Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations. While MD provides the time-resolved trajectories and thermodynamic properties of large-scale systems, quantum kernels can be used to resolve the specific energetic contributions or optimization subproblems that drive molecular recognition and self-assembly. For peptide research, particularly in the design of antimicrobial and cyclic peptides, this synergy allows for a multi-scale perspective where quantum algorithms guide the optimization of sequences that are subsequently validated through classical atomistic simulations. This PhD dissertation explores this intersection, leveraging the unique capabilities of quantum optimization to address the challenges of molecular design in complex environments such as lipid membranes.


\section{Dissertation Structure}

This PhD dissertation is structured into seven main sections and one supplementary section. The manuscript first delves into the pathophysiological context behind the work, introducing the CAIn Triangle (Cancer, Aging, and Infection) and the challenge of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). It then presents Antimicrobial Peptides (AMPs) and the computational techniques used to study them. After that, the objectives of this research are presented, followed by a technical explanation of the materials and methods. Subsequently, the scientific results are presented as a compendium of articles, comprising the most relevant publications and manuscripts related to this thesis, divided into two results sections. Finally, the contributions are discussed as a whole in order to draw a general list of conclusions. In particular, the document is structured as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Introduction}: The present section. It introduces the pathophysiological links between cancer, aging, and infection, identifying lipid asymmetry as a common underlying factor. It proposes AMPs as potential candidates to target these specific lipid signatures. Additionally, it provides an overview of the two main computational paradigms used in this work: Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations coupled with enhanced sampling, and Quantum Computing algorithms for molecular prediction and design.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis and Objectives}: This section sets the main hypothesis that drives the research conducted in this thesis. Moreover, it provides a detailed description of the multi-scale objectives that have to be achieved to test the proposed hypothesis, ranging from thermodynamic characterization to quantum sequence optimization.
    \item \textbf{Materials and Methods}: This section describes in detail the computational tools used in this research. It first focuses on classical MD and Metadynamics, providing technical insights into the physical foundations and the calculation of free energy surfaces. Then, it delves into Quantum Computing, describing the variational models (e.g., VQE) and optimization algorithms (e.g., QAOA) featured in the research.
    \item \textbf{Results: Published Articles}: This section includes the articles published during the thesis and, when applicable, the corresponding supporting information. The articles are presented in their original format, including the discussion of the results obtained in each research work. This section covers the conceptualization of the CAIn Triangle, the biophysical characterization of AMP-membrane interactions using advanced sampling, and the initial evaluation of quantum algorithms for peptide folding prediction on simplified lattice models.
    \item \textbf{Unpublished Results}: This section encompasses the work that could not be published before the presentation of the thesis. It details the transition towards combinatorial sequence optimization and the development of a physics-based Hamiltonian for \textit{de novo} peptide design. Notably, it introduces \textbf{Helix Design Studio}, an open-source software platform that integrates these theoretical and computational models to automate the rational design of $\alpha$-helical peptides in heterogeneous membrane environments. The corresponding software registration forms and licenses are attached in the Appendix.
    \item \textbf{General Discussion}: This section includes a general overview of the results achieved in this thesis, highlighting the main outcomes of each stage and how they integrate into the general purpose of bridging computational biophysics and quantum optimization.
    \item \textbf{Conclusions}: Presents a summary of the key conclusions drawn from the research carried out in this thesis.
\end{itemize}
