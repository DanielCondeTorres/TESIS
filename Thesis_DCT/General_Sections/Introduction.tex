\section{Biomedical context: cancer, aging, and infection}
Cancer, aging, and infectious diseases are among the major challenges facing modern biomedicine and global healthcare systems~\cite{WHO2024TopCausesDeath,GBD2021CausesOfDeath}. Together, they account for a substantial proportion of global morbidity and mortality, while their clinical and socioeconomic burden continues to increase owing to population aging, longer life expectancy, and the growing prevalence of chronic diseases and comorbidities~\cite{GBD2021CausesOfDeath}.

Historically, these conditions have been investigated as distinct pathological entities~\cite{Dolan2025}. However, accumulating evidence from molecular biology, immunology, and epidemiological studies indicates that cancer, aging, and infection are closely interconnected through common molecular, cellular, and immunological mechanisms~\cite{Hong2019}. These interactions influence disease susceptibility, progression, therapeutic response, and clinical outcome, suggesting that their integrated study provides a more comprehensive framework for understanding human pathophysiology~\cite{biology14010017}.

Advances in systems biology, multi-omics technologies, and precision medicine have further strengthened this concept by revealing that these conditions share multiple biological processes, including chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, genomic instability, epigenetic alterations, metabolic reprogramming, and changes in membrane lipid composition~\cite{LopezOtin2023,hannah,Medzhitov2012}. These shared mechanisms not only explain many of the reciprocal interactions among cancer, aging, and infection but also highlight common molecular pathways that may serve as biomarkers or therapeutic targets. Consequently, the integrated investigation of these processes has become an important area of translational biomedicine, promoting a systems-level perspective on the mechanisms that underlie health and disease~\cite{LopezOtin2023,kennedy2014geroscience}.


\subsection{The Cancer–Aging–Infection Triangle: interconnected pathological processes}

The Cancer–Aging–Infection (CAIn) Triangle, introduced in previous work included in this thesis, provides a conceptual framework for capturing and integrating these complex relationships, conceptualizing cancer, aging, and infection not as isolated pathological entities, but as three interdependent vertices of a biological network characterized by reciprocal feedback loops and shared pathogenic mechanisms~\cite{DANI_LIPIDOS}. Within this integrative framework, each vertex does not exist in isolation; rather, each simultaneously influences and is influenced by the others, generating multidirectional interactions that determine disease initiation, accelerate progression, and ultimately shape clinical outcomes~\cite{aging_and_cancer,vance2017listening,pawelec2017immunosenescence,wang2022immunosenescence}.


\subsubsection{The Cancer--Infection axis}

Within the CAIn framework, the interplay between cancer and infectious diseases constitutes a highly characterized, bidirectional paradigm. On one hand, persistent infections act as potent drivers of carcinogenesis by instigating chronic inflammation, disrupting tissue homeostasis, and inducing genomic instability~\cite{Greten2019}. Furthermore, specific oncogenic pathogens can directly hijack host cellular regulatory pathways to drive malignant transformation~\cite{deMartel2020}. Conversely, the systemic immunosuppression elicited by both the neoplastic process itself and standard anti-cancer therapies drastically increases patient vulnerability to opportunistic infections~\cite{Rolston2017}. Ultimately, this reciprocal dynamic establishes a deleterious feedback loop that exacerbates clinical severity and complicates therapeutic management throughout disease progression~\cite{Diakos2014}.

\subsubsection{The Aging--Infection axis}

The Aging--Infection axis is fundamentally governed by immunosenescence, the progressive deterioration of immune competence associated with advanced age~\cite{inflammaging}. This age-related attenuation across both innate and adaptive immune compartments severely compromises pathogen clearance, thereby amplifying both the incidence and severity of infectious diseases~\cite{NikolichZugich2018}. Conversely, chronic or latent infections exert a persistent antigenic load that provokes sustained immune activation and systemic, low-grade inflammation~\cite{inflammaging}. This continuous inflammatory state not only drives tissue damage but also accelerates biological aging and precipitates the onset of age-related morbidities~\cite{Muller2019}. Consequently, profound immune dysregulation lies at the mechanistic core of this axis, continuously reinforcing the vulnerability of the aging host~\cite{inflammaging}.

\subsubsection{The Cancer--Aging axis}

Cancer and aging are inextricably linked by a shared spectrum of time-dependent biological alterations~\cite{Aunan2017}. At the molecular and cellular levels, both conditions converge on common hallmarks, such as genomic instability, epigenetic drift, mitochondrial dysfunction, loss of proteostasis, chronic inflammation, and cellular senescence~\cite{LopezOtin2023}. Paradoxically, while mechanisms like senescence initially evolve as tumor-suppressive barriers to preserve tissue integrity, their chronic manifestation in older tissues generates a pro-inflammatory microenvironment that actively fuels malignant transformation and tumor progression~\cite{Campisi2013,Aunan2017}. Coupled with the age-driven decline in immune surveillance, these overlapping trajectories underscore how the biological aging process intrinsically primes the physiological landscape for tumorigenesis~\cite{Campisi2013}.






\subsection{Chronic inflammation and lipid dysregulation as shared pathophysiological features}

Although cancer, aging, and infection are characterized by distinct biological processes and clinical manifestations, their interdependence is sustained, at least in part, by convergent molecular mechanisms. Among these, chronic inflammation and dysregulation of lipid metabolism and membrane composition emerge as particularly relevant, as they are implicated across the three vertices of the CAIn Triangle~\cite{DANI_LIPIDOS,Montoliu2014,Bowman2019,Szlasa2020,Fu2021}. Chronic inflammation promotes persistent immune activation and dysfunction, cumulative tissue damage, and progressive loss of cellular homeostasis, thereby contributing to pathological processes associated with cancer, aging, and infection~\cite{inflammaging,Diakos2014,Furman2019}. In parallel, alterations in lipid metabolism and membrane composition can modulate cellular signaling, membrane dynamics, receptor function, and host--pathogen interactions, with consequences for tumorigenesis, age-related cellular dysfunction, and susceptibility to infection~\cite{Casares2019,Montoliu2014,Bowman2019,Szlasa2020,Fu2021}. The convergence of these processes provides a mechanistic basis for understanding the reciprocal links among the three conditions and highlights shared biological vulnerabilities that may be relevant for biomarker discovery, risk stratification, and therapeutic intervention~\cite{DANI_LIPIDOS}.







\section{Biological membranes as therapeutic targets: linking lipid dysregulation to the Cancer-Aging-Infection framework}

As established within our analysis of the CAIn model, chronic inflammation and lipid dysregulation emerge as two fundamental molecular processes that mechanistically link cancer, aging, and infection ~\cite{Diakos2014,Furman2019,Casares2019}. While inflammatory signaling pathways have received substantial research attention, the therapeutic potential of lipid membranes (whose composition and organization are tightly constrained by biophysical principles) has remained comparatively underexploited~\cite{Casares2019}. This section examines the structural and functional principles of biological membranes, establishing the mechanistic foundation for understanding how perturbations in lipid composition contribute to pathogenesis across all three vertices of the CAIn Triangle~\cite{Montoliu2014,Bowman2019,Szlasa2020,Fu2021} and how membrane-targeting strategies offer promise as broad-spectrum therapeutic interventions ~\cite{Casares2019,Fu2021}.

\subsection{Lipid bilayer organization and self-assembly}

Few structural features are as universally conserved across all known forms of life as the cell membrane. Rather than being viewed simply as passive, hydrophobic boundaries that enclose the cytoplasm, these lipidic scaffolds constitute the fundamental structural and functional building blocks of biological systems~\cite{gould2018membranes}. Their molecular architecture determines not only membrane integrity but also the physicochemical properties that regulate molecular recognition, cellular signalling, and protein function~\cite{lipidomics_1}.

Among the vast diversity of species that compose biological membranes, phospholipids are by far the most abundant~\cite{fosfolipidos_abundantes}. Structurally, a phospholipid consists of a polar, phosphate-containing head group linked to a hydrophobic region~\cite{fosfolipids}. This framework typically relies on a glycerol backbone esterified to two hydrophobic fatty acyl chains (in glycerophospholipids), or a sphingoid base that links to a single acyl chain while providing the second hydrocarbon tail itself (in sphingolipids) (Figure~\ref{fig:Lipids})~\cite{goni2006biophysics,van2008membrane}. The fatty acid chains profoundly condition the mechanical stability and fluidity of the membrane: longer, saturated chains favor tight packing and rigidity, whereas the kinks introduced by unsaturations reduce packing and increase fluidity~\cite{van2008membrane}.

The amphipathic nature of phospholipids, combining hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions, governs their spontaneous aggregation in aqueous environments. This self-assembly process is primarily driven by the hydrophobic effect and is therefore fundamentally entropy-driven~\cite{self-assembly}. In the liquid phase, water molecules form a dynamic and highly interconnected network of hydrogen bonds~\cite{hydrogen_network}. The introduction of nonpolar acyl chains disrupts this network, forcing adjacent water molecules to reorganize into more ordered hydration structures around the hydrophobic surfaces~\cite{hidrofobic_effect}. This increased molecular ordering reduces the entropy of the system, making the dispersed state thermodynamically unfavorable~\cite{ENTROPIA_1,ENTROPIA_2}. Consequently, lipid molecules spontaneously self-assemble by clustering their hydrophobic tails within the interior of the aggregate while exposing their hydrophilic head groups to the surrounding aqueous environment, thereby minimizing the contact between nonpolar moieties and water and restoring the entropy of the solvent~\cite{self-assembly}. This arrangement minimizes the exposed nonpolar surface area, maximizing the entropy of the surrounding water while stabilizing the resulting structure, the lipid bilayer, through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions at the membrane-water interface~\cite{self-assembly}.

Biological membranes are not static, homogeneous sheets, but highly organized two-dimensional fluids~\cite{Singer1972,Nicolson2014}. A critical feature of healthy mammalian cell membranes is their pronounced transversal asymmetry. The outer leaflet, facing the extracellular environment, is predominantly enriched in cholesterol (CHOL) zwitterionic phosphatidylcholine (PC) and sphingomyelin (SM), presenting a neutral surface. Conversely, the inner leaflet harbors the majority of phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) and the anionic phosphatidylserine (PS), conferring a net negative charge to the cytosolic face~\cite{van2008membrane}. This asymmetry is actively maintained by ATP-dependent lipid transporters, such as flippases and floppases, and is essential for numerous cellular processes, including signal transduction and vesicle formation~\cite{Holthuis2014}.

Importantly, the precise regulation of this lipid scaffold means that its alteration is profoundly tied to pathogenesis. This structural reality provides the biophysical foundation for the CAIn framework introduced previously~\cite{Casares2019}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infection:} The cytoplasmic membranes of bacteria differ fundamentally from those of mammals. For instance, the membranes of Gram-negative bacteria like \textit{Escherichia coli} are predominantly composed of PE and anionic phosphatidylglycerol (PG).This confers a strong negative charge to their external surface, a stark contrast to the neutral exterior of healthy human cells~\cite{van2008membrane,bacterial_bilayer_1}.
    \item \textbf{Cancer:} In many pathological conditions, including cancer, the tightly regulated phospholipid asymmetry of the mammalian plasma membrane becomes disrupted as a result of altered lipid transporter activity, leading to the externalization of the anionic phospholipid phosphatidylserine (PS) on the outer leaflet of the membrane~\cite{van2008membrane,Nagata2016,Szlasa2020}.
    While in normal physiology this externalization serves as an apoptotic signal for macrophage clearance, in tumor cells it acts as a mechanism of immunosuppression and provides the external membrane with a net negative charge~\cite{Szlasa2020,Fu2021}.
    \item \textbf{Aging:} Aging represents another critical physiological context characterized by progressive alterations in the lipid profile of the cytoplasmic membrane. These age-induced modifications are highly complex and variable, strongly depending on factors such as the specific tissue being studied and the biological sex of the individual~\cite{ref42}. Despite this heterogeneity, profiling these lipidomic shifts is essential. Variations in specific lipid concentrations, fluctuations in cholesterol (CHOL) content, and increased lipid oxidation are closely associated with the onset of numerous age-related diseases~\cite{ref43,ref44}. From a biophysical perspective, these biochemical alterations severely disrupt membrane fluidity, lateral domain organization, and signal transduction, ultimately driving cellular senescence and contributing to the broader pathology of aging~\cite{Montoliu2014}.
\end{itemize}

Ultimately, these profound differences in lipid composition and bilayer architecture provide a compelling mechanistic rationale~\cite{van2008membrane}. By exploiting these specific biophysical signatures, membrane-active therapies can be designed to selectively target the aberrant or foreign membranes that drive the pathologies of the CAIn triad ~\cite{Casares2019,Fu2021}.

\subsection{Membrane organization in healthy mammalian cells}

Building upon the structural principles outlined previously, the tightly regulated lipid organization of healthy mammalian cells serves as the biophysical reference against which the selectivity of novel therapeutics must be evaluated~\cite{POPC_MEMBRANE,Sani2015}. In this homeostatic state, the strict transversal asymmetry of the bilayer creates a robust barrier against exogenous membrane-active molecules~\cite{Ikeda2006}. Because the outer leaflet is densely populated by zwitterionic PC and SM, the cellular surface presents a net-neutral electrostatic charge to the extracellular environment~\cite{bilayer_asimetry_1}. 

Furthermore, the high cholesterol content of mammalian plasma membranes allows cholesterol molecules to intercalate between phospholipid acyl chains, restricting their conformational mobility and increasing lipid packing, membrane order, and mechanical stability of the bilayer~\cite{van2008membrane}. Together, this charge neutrality and high mechanical cohesion minimize the thermodynamic propensity for amphipathic or cationic molecules (such as antimicrobial or anticancer peptides) to bind and insert into healthy membranes, thereby preventing off-target toxicity~\cite{lipidomics_4}.

\subsection{Microbial and pathological membrane remodeling}

Deviations from this healthy baseline, whether through evolutionary divergence in microbes or pathological remodeling in cancer, expose fundamental biophysical vulnerabilities~\cite{BACTERIA_MEMBRANE,CANCER_MEMBRANE}. 

In bacterial pathogens, the predominance of anionic phospholipids such as phosphatidylglycerol (PG) and cardiolipin gives rise to a highly electronegative cell envelope with biophysical properties distinct from those of mammalian membranes~\cite{bacterial_bilayer_1,bacterial_bilayer_2,Geiger2013}. This lipid composition not only supports essential cellular processes, including membrane organization and molecular transport~\cite{bacterial_bilayer_3}, but also promotes the electrostatic attraction of cationic antimicrobial peptides. Although bacteria can remodel their membrane lipid composition in response to environmental stress, the predominance of anionic phospholipids remains a conserved feature across many bacterial species~\cite{bacteria_bilayer_4,Vance2015}.

Similarly, malignant transformation disrupts lipid homeostasis, creating membrane alterations that can be exploited as therapeutic targets. One hallmark of many cancer cells is the externalization of phosphatidylserine (PS), which under physiological conditions serves as an “eat-me” signal for macrophage-mediated clearance of apoptotic cells~\cite{cancer_bilayer_1}. In tumors, however, PS exposure contributes to the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment~\cite{cancer_bilayer_2,cancer_bilayer_3}. This pathological remodeling also confers a more negatively charged cell surface, thereby enhancing the electrostatic attraction of cationic anticancer peptides. In addition, cancer cells frequently exhibit dysregulated cholesterol homeostasis, resulting in alterations in membrane organization and biophysical properties that vary according to tumor type and stage~\cite{Szlasa2020}.

\subsection{Physicochemical basis of membrane selectivity}

These profound differences highlight that the lipid composition of membranes is not merely a structural perimeter, but a highly tractable molecular target~\cite{DANI_LIPIDOS,lipidomics_1}. The physicochemical basis of selectivity relies on exploiting these exact disparities: cationic, amphipathic therapies are electrostatically drawn to the anionic surfaces of bacteria and cancer cells, while subsequently exploiting their altered fluidity (e.g., lower CHOL content) to insert into and disrupt the bilayer, all while being repelled by the neutral, tightly packed membranes of healthy cells~\cite{torres2019peptide}. 

This membrane-targeting strategy offers several advantages over conventional pharmacological approaches~\cite{membrane_therapy}. Traditional therapeutics typically act by targeting specific protein receptors or metabolic enzymes, which can readily acquire point mutations that confer antimicrobial or antineoplastic resistance~\cite{holohan2013cancer}. In contrast, resistance to membrane-active molecules generally requires extensive remodeling of membrane lipid composition and physicochemical properties, changes that are often constrained by substantial evolutionary and fitness costs because membrane integrity is essential for cell viability~\cite{lipidomics_2}. Consequently, advances in lipidomics have enabled the rational design of molecules that selectively recognize conserved membrane physicochemical signatures, providing a promising framework for the development of more robust therapies with a reduced propensity for resistance~\cite{lipidomics_4}.

\section{Antimicrobial peptides as membrane-active molecules}

The recognition that membrane composition and organization constitute promising therapeutic targets has stimulated growing interest in membrane-active compounds capable of selectively interacting with pathological cells. Among these, Antimicrobial Peptides (AMPs) have emerged as one of the most extensively investigated classes of membrane-targeting molecules because they exploit physicochemical differences between healthy and diseased membranes rather than highly specific molecular receptors~\cite{WEISBERG2026102781}.

As part of the innate immune system of virtually all living organisms, AMPs are produced by a wide variety of animals, plants, fungi, and microorganisms as a first line of defense against invading pathogens~\cite{Zasloff2002}. Initially identified for their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, these peptides are now recognized as multifunctional molecules that also display antiviral, antifungal, immunomodulatory, and anticancer properties~\cite{Midura-Nowaczek2014}. Their biological activity largely arises from their ability to exploit fundamental physicochemical differences between healthy and pathological membranes, particularly variations in lipid composition, membrane asymmetry, and surface electrostatics, enabling preferential interactions with membranes enriched in negatively charged phospholipids~\cite{Midura-Nowaczek2014}.

\subsection{Biological origin and functional diversity}

AMPs constitute one of the most ancient and evolutionarily conserved components of innate immunity, having been identified across virtually all domains of life, from bacteria and fungi to plants, invertebrates, and vertebrates~\cite{Hancock2006,Kumar2018,fermentation10110540,microorganisms13112574}. Despite their remarkable structural diversity, they share the common function of providing a rapid first line of defense against invading microorganisms while also participating in immune regulation, wound healing, and the maintenance of tissue homeostasis~\cite{Hancock2006,Kumar2018}.

The remarkable diversity of naturally occurring AMPs is reflected in the Antimicrobial Peptide Database (APD6)\footnote{\url{https://aps.unmc.edu/}}, which, as of June 2026, contains more than 6,600 experimentally reported peptide sequences, encompassing molecules with diverse physicochemical properties, structures, biological origins, and mechanisms of action~\cite{Wang2016}. This broad molecular repertoire makes the establishment of a universal classification challenging and highlights the need to understand how sequence, structure, and physicochemical properties determine biological activity~\cite{Huan2020}.

Beyond their biological relevance, AMPs have attracted considerable attention as potential therapeutic agents in response to the growing global crisis of Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)~\cite{WEISBERG2026102781,ho2025antimicrobial}. The rapid emergence of multidrug-resistant pathogens has progressively reduced the efficacy of conventional antibiotics, creating an urgent need for alternative therapeutic strategies~\cite{salam2023antimicrobial,macnair2024alternative}. Unlike most small-molecule antibiotics, which typically target specific intracellular pathways, AMPs generally exert their activity through rapid, physicochemically driven, and often multitarget mechanisms that make the emergence of resistance comparatively less frequent~\cite{microorganisms13112574,Huan2020}. Consequently, AMPs are increasingly regarded as promising candidates not only for the treatment of infectious diseases but also for membrane-targeted therapies against other pathological conditions, including cancer~\cite{torres2020wasp}.






\subsection{Sequence, physicochemical properties and structural classes}

The biological functionality of an AMP is intrinsically linked to its primary amino acid sequence and resulting three-dimensional topology. Structurally, these peptides represent a diverse class of small oligomers, typically comprising 10 to 100 amino acid residues~\cite{Zasloff2002,numero_aa_amps,doi:10.1128/cmr.00056-05}. Their therapeutic activity is primarily governed by two biophysical pillars~\cite{Mookherjee2020}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cationicity:} The vast majority of AMPs possess a net positive charge (typically ranging from +2 to +13), primarily due to the presence of Lysine (Lys, K) and Arginine (Arg, R) residues~\cite{numero_aa_amps}. This cationic nature is crucial for the initial electrostatic recruitment and selective interaction with the negatively charged membranes of invading pathogens, rather than the neutrally charged membranes of eukaryotic host cells~\cite{Zasloff2002,Mookherjee2020,Zhang2021}.
    \item \textbf{Amphipathicity:} AMPs exhibit a highly regulated balance between hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions, usually presenting a hydrophobicity of around 30\% to 60\%~\cite{hidrofobicidad_amps,Yeaman2003}. This dual nature is essential for their insertion and partitioning into the non-polar lipid core of biological membranes~\cite{Deb2026}.
\end{itemize}

Based on the secondary structure they acquire at the target interface, AMPs are generally classified into four archetypes (Figure~\ref{fig:AMPClass})~\cite{fermentation10110540,doi:10.1128/cmr.00056-05}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{$\alpha$-helical peptides:} The most abundant in nature (e.g., magainins, pleurocidin, LL-37). They fold into an $\alpha$-helix upon adsorption into the lipid bilayer.
    \item \textbf{$\beta$-sheet peptides:} Structures stabilized by intramolecular disulfide bridges (Cys-Cys bonds), providing high resistance to proteolytic degradation (e.g., human defensins, thanatin).
    \item \textbf{Loop/Cyclic peptides:} Structures formed via head-to-tail cyclization or single disulfide bonds (e.g., bactenecins).
    \item \textbf{Extended peptides:} Sequences that lack traditional secondary folding and are often rich in specific residues like Proline or Tryptophan (e.g., indolicidin).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Environment-dependent folding and membrane recognition}

Unlike most globular proteins, many AMPs do not adopt a stable three-dimensional structure in aqueous solution. Instead, they exist as highly flexible or intrinsically disordered molecules that undergo rapid conformational transitions upon encountering biological membranes or membrane-mimetic environments~\cite{Li2021}. This environmentally induced folding enables AMPs to maximize their amphipathic character by spatially segregating hydrophobic and positively charged residues, thereby promoting favorable interactions with lipid bilayers~\cite{torres2019peptide}.

The membrane itself therefore acts as a structural template that drives peptide folding~\cite{experiments_2}. Electrostatic attraction between the cationic residues of AMPs and negatively charged membrane lipids facilitates the initial adsorption of the peptide onto the membrane surface. Subsequently, hydrophobic interactions promote partial insertion into the lipid bilayer, stabilizing secondary structural elements such as $\alpha$-helices, $\beta$-sheets, or extended conformations depending on the peptide sequence and membrane composition~\cite{Li2021}. This structural plasticity is essential for biological activity, as the same peptide may adopt distinct conformations in solution, bacterial membranes, mammalian membranes, or membrane-mimetic systems~\cite{torres2019peptide}.

Membrane recognition is therefore governed by a delicate interplay between peptide physicochemical properties and membrane characteristics. Peptide charge, hydrophobicity, amphipathicity, sequence distribution, and conformational flexibility collectively determine membrane affinity, while lipid composition, membrane fluidity, surface charge density, CHOL content, and lipid packing dictate the accessibility and stability of peptide binding~\cite{Yeaman2003,Huan2020}. Rather than being determined by a single molecular receptor, AMP selectivity emerges from these collective physicochemical interactions~\cite{Zasloff2002}.

Because peptide folding, membrane recognition, and membrane disruption are intrinsically coupled processes, understanding the relationship between sequence, structure, and membrane interactions has become a central objective in AMP research. This relationship also underpins modern computational approaches for peptide design, where predicting environment-dependent conformations and membrane affinity is essential for developing molecules with improved therapeutic efficacy and selectivity~\cite{Deb2026}.



\subsection{Membrane disruption and non-lytic mechanisms}

While some AMPs display their antimicrobial activity by means of intracellular, non-lytic mechanisms ~\cite{Scocchi2016}, the most common modes of action involve membranolytic effects driven by their amphipathic nature (Figure~\ref{fig:AMPMOA}). The therapeutic action of membrane-targeting AMPs is achieved through a two-stage physical disruption: initial electrostatic accumulation at the membrane surface, followed by the hydrophobic partitioning of the peptide into the non-polar core, which disturbs the lipid bilayer structure and ultimately leads to cell death~\cite{Mookherjee2020,BuiThiPhuong2024}. 

Once a critical concentration of peptides is adsorbed, the membrane is compromised through several proposed models:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pore formation models (barrel-stave and toroidal-pore):} In the barrel-stave model, peptides insert perpendicularly into the membrane, aggregating such that their hydrophobic faces interact with lipid tails while their hydrophilic faces form a central aqueous channel. In the toroidal-pore model, peptides induce local curvature, causing the lipid headgroups to tilt and line the pore alongside the peptides.
    \item \textbf{Non-pore models (carpet model):} Peptides accumulate parallel to the membrane surface. Upon reaching a threshold concentration, they act like detergents, leading to the micellization and fragmentation of the bilayer without forming discrete, stable pores.
\end{itemize}

\section{Bottlenecks in AMP research and therapeutic development}

Despite this potent and physically driven mechanism of action, translating AMPs into clinical therapies remains a significant challenge. As of its latest update in December 2024 [Retrieved June 2026], the Data Repository of AMPs (DRAMP)~\cite{Ma2025} lists 35,918 entries under the query \textit{antimicrobial}. Nevertheless, only a few AMPs have reached clinical trials, and barely over 100 peptide-based drugs have been approved worldwide~\cite{Rossino2023,Pereira2024,amps_aprobados}. 

\subsection{Pharmacological and translational challenges}

The translation of AMPs from \textit{in vitro} efficacy to \textit{in vivo} clinical viability is primarily hindered by their unfavorable pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles~\cite{fracaso_amps}. A major limitation is their poor metabolic stability; native linear peptides are highly susceptible to rapid degradation by endogenous proteolytic enzymes present in blood serum, tissues, and the gastrointestinal tract~\cite{Dijksteel2021,degradacion_1,degradacion_2}. Furthermore, their \textit{in vivo} activity is frequently compromised under physiological salt concentrations. The high ionic strength of bodily fluids can shield the electrostatic interactions between cationic peptides and anionic membranes, thereby reducing membrane affinity and antimicrobial activity. Combined with their low molecular weight, which contributes to rapid renal clearance, these factors can substantially shorten the half-life and limit the systemic bioavailability of natural AMPs~\cite{fracaso_amps}.

Another critical bottleneck is the risk of off-target toxicity. Although AMPs generally exhibit a thermodynamic preference for anionic microbial or pathological membranes, this selectivity is rarely absolute~\cite{magana2020value}. At the relatively high concentrations often required to achieve systemic efficacy, many AMPs can lose their selectivity and exhibit toxicity toward host mammalian cells~\cite{Lyu2023}. This toxicity typically manifests as hemolysis (the disruption of red blood cells) or cytotoxicity toward healthy tissues, which can substantially narrow their therapeutic window and restrict many current AMP applications to topical rather than systemic administration~\cite{haney2019reassessing}.

To overcome these translational limitations, considerable efforts have focused on peptide engineering and chemical optimization. Rational design strategies aim to improve the balance between antimicrobial efficacy and host-cell toxicity while enhancing resistance to proteolytic degradation~\cite{Muttenthaler2021}. Common approaches include the incorporation of non-natural amino acids to reduce susceptibility to protease recognition and peptide cyclization, which can restrict conformational flexibility and protect the peptide termini from exopeptidase-mediated degradation~\cite{Carmona2013,Lucana2021}. However, experimentally exploring this vast chemical space remains costly and time-consuming, highlighting the need for advanced computational methods to accelerate the discovery and optimization of robust, clinically viable AMPs~\cite{SUN2026102685,robert2021resource}.

\subsection{Experimental limitations in peptide-membrane characterization}

The study of AMPs and their interactions with lipid membranes is inherently complex. Because biological membranes are highly heterogeneous and dynamic, experimental characterization often relies on simplified membrane-mimetic systems, including surfactant micelles, homogeneous or binary lipid mixtures, and helix-stabilizing co-solvents such as 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol (TFE)~\cite{paper_paula,experiments_1,experiments_2,experiments_3}. These simplified environments facilitate controlled and reproducible biophysical measurements, but they can also limit the extent to which experimental observations can be extrapolated to physiological conditions. In particular, the structure of membrane-targeting AMPs, especially their $\alpha$-helical content, can be highly sensitive to the surrounding membrane environment~\cite{experiment_4,experiment_5}. Consequently, differences in membrane composition and other experimental parameters can lead to substantially different structural and functional responses, complicating comparisons across studies.

Additional limitations arise from experimental approaches that require modification of the peptide or membrane system. Fluorescence spectroscopy is widely used to investigate peptide binding, membrane association, aggregation, and membrane permeabilization, either by exploiting intrinsic fluorophores such as tryptophan or through the incorporation of extrinsic fluorescent tags~\cite{fluo_1,fluo_3,fluo_4}. However, covalent attachment of fluorescent probes can alter the physicochemical properties of the peptide and consequently modify its interaction with the membrane~\cite{fluo_2}. Similarly, vesicle leakage assays provide a convenient means of quantifying membrane permeabilization, but their interpretation depends on the properties of the fluorescent probes and the experimental configuration used to monitor leakage~\cite{fluo_3,fluo_4}. Together with the substantial diversity of membrane-mimetic systems and experimental protocols reported in the literature, these factors make direct comparisons between experimental measurements challenging and complicate the extrapolation of \textit{in vitro} observations to physiological environments.

Beyond the challenges associated with reproducing the native membrane environment, individual biophysical techniques provide only partial information about peptide-membrane interactions. Indeed, the structural characterization of membrane-active peptides is experimentally challenging, and no single experimental technique can provide a complete structural description of the peptide-membrane system~\cite{general_1}. Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy is widely used to characterize the overall secondary structure of peptides, whereas nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy can provide detailed structural information and enable the determination of peptide conformations under appropriate experimental conditions~\cite{cd_1,cd_2,nmr_1,nmr_2}. However, CD measurements are based on the differential absorption of left- and right-circularly polarized light and provide ensemble-averaged information rather than resolving individual molecular events~\cite{cd_4,cd_3}. Moreover, quantitative estimation of secondary-structure content from CD spectra typically relies on spectral analysis and reference datasets, rather than providing direct atomic-level structural information~\cite{cd_3}.

Other techniques provide complementary information but are subject to their own limitations. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) can visualize membrane morphology and characterize topological defects and pore formation associated with AMP activity~\cite{afm_1,afm_2}. Nevertheless, AFM studies of peptide-induced membrane disruption are commonly performed on supported lipid bilayers, meaning that the membrane is constrained by a solid substrate and may therefore not reproduce the geometry and mechanical environment of free-standing vesicles in solution~\cite{afm_6}. Calorimetric approaches, in turn, can quantify thermodynamic parameters associated with peptide-membrane binding, providing valuable information about membrane affinity and the energetics of membrane interactions~\cite{afm_4}. However, such measurements yield ensemble thermodynamic observables and do not directly resolve the individual molecular events underlying membrane recognition, insertion, and disruption~\cite{afm_4}.

Taken together, these complementary experimental approaches provide valuable information about the structural, morphological, and thermodynamic properties of AMP-membrane interactions. However, each technique probes only specific aspects of the system and under particular experimental conditions~\cite{general_1}. The resulting measurements therefore provide complementary views of a highly dynamic process, while the molecular events connecting peptide structure, membrane binding, insertion, and disruption remain difficult to resolve experimentally~\cite{afm_1,afm_2,cd_3}.

\subsection{Challenges for rational and environment-specific design}

Ultimately, while combining diverse spectroscopic, microscopic, and thermodynamic techniques is essential to obtain a comprehensive experimental picture of AMP activity, a critical gap remains. No single conventional experimental method can independently provide a complete, continuous, time-resolved, atomic-level description of the dynamic processes underlying peptide folding, membrane binding, insertion, and disruption~\cite{general_1,afm_1,afm_2}. This limitation poses a fundamental challenge for the rational and environment-specific design of AMPs, as the relationship between peptide sequence, structure, membrane composition, and antimicrobial activity cannot be fully captured through static or ensemble-averaged measurements. Advanced computational approaches can therefore provide complementary molecular-level insights, helping to bridge the gap between experimentally accessible observables and the dynamic mechanisms governing peptide-lipid interactions~\cite{dror2012biomolecular}.


\section{Computational approaches to peptide-membrane systems}

To overcome the limitations inherent to experimental characterization, \textit{*in silico*} approaches have emerged as indispensable tools in biophysical research~\cite{dror2012biomolecular}. As the capabilities of these methods are closely linked to available computational resources, continuous advances in high-performance computing have expanded the scope of molecular simulations, enabling the study of increasingly complex biological systems at higher spatial and temporal resolution~\cite{Melo2023}. Computational chemistry provides a rigorous, physics-based framework for investigating the structural, thermodynamic, and kinetic properties of biomolecular systems~\cite{Karplus2002,Hollingsworth2018}.

Within the context of AMPs, understanding peptide-membrane interactions requires addressing multiple scales of complexity. At the molecular level, the continuous and time-dependent behavior of peptides in contact with lipid membranes can be investigated using classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations~\cite{avances_3}. However, the vast conformational and sequence space associated with peptide folding and design presents a substantial computational challenge, particularly when exploring large numbers of candidate sequences or long-timescale processes~\cite{robert2021resource}. These limitations have motivated the development and application of complementary computational paradigms, including Artificial Intelligence (AI) and, more recently, Quantum Computing (QC)~\cite{avances_1,Jumper2021}. In this thesis, we explore this multiscale computational landscape by integrating advanced classical simulations with emerging quantum algorithms to connect molecular-level mechanisms with predictive approaches for AMP design.


\subsection{Molecular dynamics as a molecular-resolution framework}

Unlike conventional experimental techniques that yield macroscopic or time-averaged data, the simulation of systems involving peptides and lipid bilayers through MD provides continuous, atomistic trajectories of all particles in the system ~\cite{dror2012biomolecular,Sousa2010}. By solving the classical equations of motion over time, MD effectively acts as a computational microscope, allowing the study of peptide adsorption, interfacial folding, and membrane disruption at a level of spatial and temporal detail far beyond what can be achieved experimentally ~\cite{TIELEMAN1997235}.

The first MD simulation dates back to 1957, when Wainwright and Alder simulated systems of hard spheres ~\cite{alder1957phase}. The next step was taken by Rahman in 1964, who simulated liquid argon using a Lennard-Jones potential~\cite{rahman1964correlations}. In 1967, Verlet proposed a stable numerical integration scheme for Newton's equations, an approach still widely used today~\cite{Verlet1967}. In 1971, Rahman and Stillinger published the first MD study on a model of liquid water~\cite{Rahman1971}, and the definitive breakthrough for biology came in 1977, when a group led by Karplus published the first MD simulation of a simple protein~\cite{McCammon1977}. During the 1980s, significant algorithmic advances enabled MD to accurately sample physiological conditions, driven by the introduction of robust thermostats and barostats by Andersen~\cite{Andersen1980}, Parrinello and Rahman ~\cite{Parrinello1980,Parrinello1981}, Nosé~\cite{Nose1984}, and Hoover~\cite{Hoover1985}. Concurrently, conceptual breakthroughs in dimensionality reduction~\cite{Levitt1975} and the integration of quantum and classical mechanics (Quantum Mechanics/Molecular Mechanics (QM/MM))~\cite{WARSHEL1976227,Warshel1972} expanded the applicability of these simulations to complex biological reactions. This continuous exponential growth in both methodological rigor and computational scale ultimately culminated in the 2013 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Martin Karplus, Arieh Warshel, and Michael Levitt for the development of multiscale models for complex chemical systems~\cite{nobel_chemistry_2013,royal_swedish_academy_2013}.

Building on this historical legacy, the field is currently undergoing a significant paradigm shift driven by the integration of AI~\cite{cecilia_1}. While traditional empirical force fields, particularly at the Coarse-Grained (CG) level, suffer from reduced resolution and fixed functional forms that limit their accuracy, modern Machine Learning (ML) architectures offer a powerful alternative~\cite{cecilia_1}. By training deep neural networks on massive datasets of high-resolution all-atom simulations, state-of-the-art ML potentials can construct bottom-up, transferable CG models that successfully bridge the gap between atomistic accuracy and CG computational efficiency~\cite{charron2025navigating}. This breakthrough enables the predictive exploration of complex protein landscapes and rare conformational transitions at a fraction of the traditional computational cost, marking the next frontier in biophysical simulations~\cite{charron2025navigating}.

Classical MD computes the motion of particles by integrating Newton’s equations of motion~\cite{leyesnewton}. The accuracy of these simulations depends fundamentally on the force field (FF), which provides the parameterized empirical equations for bonded and non-bonded interactions. The choice of FF is critical; if the selected model fails to accurately capture the relevant non-covalent interactions (e.g., specific peptide-lipid affinities), the resulting trajectories will diverge from reality and fail to represent the physical system~\cite{braun2018best,force_field_protocol}.

\subsection{Multiscale molecular representations}

To mitigate the immense computational cost of calculating forces for thousands of water molecules, early methodologies, tracing back to the pioneering dipole models of Warshel and Levitt~\cite{WARSHEL1976227}, relied heavily on implicit solvent representations. These approaches treat the solvent as a continuous polarizable medium with a macroscopic dielectric constant, effectively reducing the system's degrees of freedom~\cite{implicit_solvent_model}. However, continuum models inherently fail to capture the highly directional, discrete hydrogen-bonding networks established between the peptide backbone and individual water molecules~\cite{implicit_vs_explicit}. In the context of AMPs, this limitation is critical: the dynamic competition between peptide-water and intramolecular peptide-peptide hydrogen bonds is the fundamental driving force governing their characteristic environment-conditioned folding (e.g., the transition from a disordered coil in solution to an amphipathic $\alpha$-helix at the lipid interface)~\cite{white1999membrane}.

Consequently, atomistic MD simulations of peptide–membrane interactions commonly employ explicit solvent and atomistically resolved lipid bilayers, enabling a detailed description of peptide–lipid and peptide–water interactions~\cite{hypothesis}. While this explicit representation accurately captures the complex interfacial mechanics, lipid hydration shells, and thermodynamic forces governing peptide adsorption~\cite{Kabelka2021,Marrink2019}, it comes at a severe price. The explicit inclusion of water and lipid molecules typically accounts for over 90\% of the atoms in the simulation box, drastically amplifying the computational burden and severely restricting the accessible timescales for observing spontaneous folding and membrane disruption events~\cite{Frenkel2023,implicit_solvent_model,martini2}.

To bridge this critical timescale gap and capture the slow, complex phenomena relevant to AMPs, such as membrane partitioning, spontaneous pore formation, and peptide aggregation, researchers typically rely on two complementary computational strategies: reducing the system's degrees of freedom or algorithmically accelerating the exploration of the energy landscape~\cite{GF2016,Calvelo2019,Xandre2021,Conde2022}.

To access longer timescales and larger spatial dimensions, CG approaches, such as the widely used Martini force field, map groups of approximately four heavy atoms into a single interaction site (bead), significantly reducing the number of particles in the system and computational cost~\cite{martini2}. This dimensional reduction allows for simulations that are orders of magnitude faster than atomistic MD ~\cite{Claro2020,Claro2021}. However, while CG models are exceptionally well-suited to capture collective phenomena such as global membrane remodeling and lipid sorting, their reduced resolution inherently compromises the accurate description of specific atomic-level events~\cite{paper_alfonso}. Crucially for AMPs, the loss of explicit hydrogen atoms means that highly dynamic, environment-conditioned folding transitions (e.g., the coil-to-helix transition upon membrane contact) cannot be spontaneously described without additional artificial constraints, such as elastic networks. This limitation leaves a crucial gap between atomistic accuracy and CG scalability~\cite{limitation_martini_1,charron2025navigating,SIRAH}.

\subsection{Enhanced sampling and thermodynamic characterization}

While CG models significantly extend accessible timescales, deep free-energy basins and high kinetic barriers can still trap the system in local minima~\cite{limitaciones_md}. Consequently, the spontaneous observation of rare events (such as deep membrane insertion, specific lipid recruitment, or pore formation) remains highly challenging. To systematically overcome these barriers, regardless of the chosen resolution (atomistic or CG), MD is frequently coupled with Enhanced Sampling techniques~\cite{limitation_martini_1,limitation_martini_2,steered_md_1,steered_md_2,Richardson2024}. These methods manipulate the thermodynamic ensembles or the underlying potential energy surface to accelerate the exploration of the conformational space and reconstruct the free-energy landscapes. In the context of AMP-membrane interactions, where such combinations have successfully unveiled the specificity of peptide orientations~\cite{samson_umbrella_sampling}, the most prominent techniques include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Replica-Exchange MD (REMD):} A generalized ensemble method that runs multiple parallel simulations at varying temperatures, allowing configurations to swap and preventing the system from becoming trapped in local energy minima~\cite{replica}.
    \item \textbf{Metadynamics:} A technique that adds a history-dependent, Gaussian biasing potential to the Free Energy Surface (FES), effectively \textit{filling} the local minima visited by the system and forcing it to explore new conformations along a set of Collective Variables (CVs)   ~\cite{Laio2002,plumed,opes,Barducci2008}.
    \item \textbf{Umbrella Sampling:} A stratification method that restrains the system along a predefined reaction coordinate (e.g., the distance between the peptide Center of Mass (COM) and the membrane core) using a harmonic bias, allowing for the extraction of the Potential of Mean Force (PMF) across the transition pathway~\cite{umbrella_sampling_origen,umbrella_sampling}.
    \item \textbf{Steered MD (SMD):} A non-equilibrium acceleration approach where time-dependent external forces are applied to pull the peptide along a specific vector or reaction coordinate~\cite{steered_md}. In AMP studies, SMD is extensively employed as a powerful tool to force membrane permeation and generate the continuous pathway of initial conformations required to seed subsequent equilibrium methods like Umbrella Sampling~\cite{steered_md_2,steered_md_1}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Computational peptide design}

Recent advances in machine-learning-based approaches, most notably AlphaFold, have achieved remarkable success in predicting protein three-dimensional structures from amino acid sequences~\cite{Jumper2021,Yang2023}. However, structure-prediction approaches such as AlphaFold are primarily designed to predict individual protein structures rather than to explicitly resolve folding pathways or the thermodynamic and kinetic properties of the conformational ensembles underlying protein dynamics~\cite{Bowman2024}. Although recent machine-learning-based generative models and force fields have emerged as promising approaches for sampling conformational ensembles and simulating protein dynamics, their ability to accurately capture environment-dependent processes and interactions with non-protein components remains an active area of research~\cite{cecilia_1,charron2025navigating}. This is particularly relevant for systems involving peptide--lipid interactions and membrane-associated conformational transitions, where the surrounding molecular environment can strongly influence protein structure and dynamics~\cite{articulo_limitacion_ia,Yang2023}.



\section{Quantum computing as an emerging paradigm for molecular optimization}\label{QC_intro}

The discovery and optimization of AMPs involve computational problems of extraordinary complexity, arising from the enormous sequence diversity, the high-dimensional conformational landscape, and the intricate physicochemical interactions governing peptide--membrane recognition~\cite{robert2021resource}. Although advances in classical computing and AI have substantially accelerated peptide design, the combinatorial nature of these problems continues to impose significant computational limitations~\cite{SUN2026102685}. In this context, QC has emerged as a promising computational paradigm capable of exploiting quantum-mechanical principles to tackle optimization and simulation problems that are difficult to address using conventional approaches. While current quantum hardware remains limited, ongoing developments suggest that quantum algorithms may become valuable complementary tools for molecular modelling, drug discovery, and biomolecular optimization~\cite{q_adv}.


\subsection{Computational complexity of conformational and sequence spaces}

A fundamental challenge in the study of AMPs arises from the enormous size of both sequence and conformational spaces~\cite{robert2021resource}. For a peptide of length \(L\), the number of possible amino acid sequences scales exponentially as \(20^L\), making exhaustive exploration infeasible even for relatively short peptides~\cite{robert2021resource}. In addition to sequence diversity, each peptide can adopt a vast number of three-dimensional conformations, which are strongly influenced by the surrounding environment, particularly in heterogeneous systems such as lipid membranes~\cite{Zasloff2002}.

This complexity is closely related to the Levinthal paradox, which highlights the apparent contradiction between the astronomical number of possible conformations and the relatively fast folding times observed in nature~\cite{Levinthal_paradox}. This paradox suggests that peptide folding is guided by structured energy landscapes rather than random exploration, yet accurately characterizing these landscapes remains a major computational challenge~\cite{Levinthal_paradox_2}.

From a computational perspective, the protein folding problem has been shown to be NP-hard under several simplified models, implying that no efficient classical algorithm is known to solve it in the general case~\cite{robert2021resource}. This intrinsic computational complexity further limits the applicability of classical methods when attempting to explore both sequence and conformational spaces in a systematic manner~\cite{Gainza2016}.

Classical approaches, such as MD simulations, are inherently constrained by accessible timescales and sampling efficiency, particularly in the presence of rare events and complex energy barriers. As a result, a comprehensive exploration of peptide–membrane interactions remains computationally prohibitive~\cite{avances_3,limitaciones_md}.

These limitations motivate the exploration of alternative computational paradigms capable of addressing the exponential and combinatorial complexity of biomolecular systems~\cite{Gainza2016}. In this context, QC offers a fundamentally different framework, in which the principles of superposition and entanglement enable the representation and manipulation of high-dimensional quantum states more naturally~\cite{robert2021resource}. Although current quantum hardware remains limited, these approaches provide a promising avenue for tackling the intrinsic complexity of peptide folding and sequence exploration problems~\cite{quantum_biology_1,quantum_biology_2}.

\subsection{Quantum information and quantum computation}

The emergence of QC represents a paradigm shift in our ability to process information, rooted in the fundamental principles of quantum mechanics. The field's conceptual origin is often traced back to Richard Feynman's 1982 observation that simulating nature (which is inherently quantum mechanical) requires a machine that operates on the same principles~\cite{feynman1982simulating}. As detailed in the seminal work of Nielsen and Chuang~\cite{nielsen2010quantum}, quantum computation transcends the limits of the classical Turing machine by utilizing qubits, which can exist in superpositions and entangled states. This allows for the exploration of a Hilbert space that grows exponentially with the number of qubits, offering a route to solve computational problems that are practically intractable for even the most powerful classical supercomputers.

\subsection{Variational quantum algorithms}

The theoretical promise of quantum advantage was first demonstrated through algorithms such as Shor's for factoring integers and Grover's for database searching~\cite{nielsen2010quantum,paper_dani}. However, the current state of technology, known as the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era~\cite{preskill2018quantum}, is characterized by devices with limited qubit counts and high error rates. Despite these limitations, the development of hybrid quantum-classical algorithms has opened new avenues for practical applications. These algorithms, such as the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)~\cite{peruzzo2014variational} and the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)~\cite{QAOA}, distribute the computational load between a quantum processor and a classical optimizer, making them remarkably robust against the noise inherent to current hardware.

QC has the potential to revolutionize chemistry and biophysics by enabling more accurate simulations of molecular systems~\cite{paper_dani}. While classical methods face significant challenges in describing the electronic correlation energy of large molecules, quantum computers can efficiently represent the underlying quantum states. Algorithms such as the VQE have already demonstrated accurate calculations of the potential energy surfaces of small molecules, highlighting the promise of QC for future quantum chemistry applications~\cite{mcclean2016theory}.

For molecular optimization, a central connection between the physical model and quantum computation is provided by the Hamiltonian. Energetic or objective functions describing a reduced molecular problem can be encoded into a quantum-compatible Hamiltonian, such that candidate solutions are represented by quantum states and favorable solutions correspond to low-energy states. In this way, simplified conformational and sequence-design models can be translated into forms suitable for quantum or hybrid quantum--classical optimization.
In the context of this thesis, these Hamiltonians do not represent a direct solution of the full electronic structure of the peptide. Instead, selected physicochemical interactions are first incorporated into reduced molecular models, which are subsequently mapped onto quantum-compatible representations. The quality of the resulting predictions therefore depends both on the physical assumptions of the model and on the performance of the optimization algorithm. Thus, in this framework, quantum computation is used primarily as an optimization strategy for reduced molecular models rather than as a direct replacement for conventional electronic-structure calculations.

\subsection{Potential applications in peptide modeling and design}

Beyond energy estimation, the application of quantum optimization to protein folding and sequence design represents one of the most exciting frontiers in the field. By mapping conformational landscapes and amino acid interactions onto a qubit space, quantum algorithms can navigate the vast discrete search spaces of molecular design more efficiently than classical heuristic searches~\cite{robert2021resource}.

QC provides a complementary computational framework to classical molecular simulation methods. While MD enables the characterization of molecular motion, membrane interactions, and thermodynamic properties across relevant spatial and temporal scales, quantum algorithms offer the potential to address electronic-structure problems whose computational cost or accuracy remains challenging for classical methods, particularly in systems involving strong electron correlation or excited states~\cite{Bidart2026}. In the context of peptide design, these complementary capabilities could be combined within a multiscale workflow, in which quantum algorithms are used to explore and optimize molecular properties, while classical atomistic simulations provide a physically detailed assessment of peptide--membrane interactions~\cite{avances_1}. This dissertation investigates this emerging intersection between quantum computing and molecular simulation, with a particular focus on the application of quantum optimization methods to peptide design in complex membrane environments. Whether these approaches can outperform state-of-the-art classical algorithms for biomolecular optimization remains an open question and must therefore be assessed through direct and appropriately controlled benchmarking.


\subsection{Current limitations of quantum computing}

Despite the remarkable advances achieved during the last decade, QC remains in an early stage of technological development~\cite{paper_dani}. Current quantum processors have demonstrated the feasibility of executing proof-of-concept algorithms and small-scale applications; however, they are still far from delivering the large-scale, fault-tolerant performance required for widespread practical use. The main limitation is not simply the number of available qubits, but the ability to operate a sufficient number of logical qubits with high fidelity, low error rates, efficient connectivity, and acceptable computational overhead~\cite{Palsberg2026,Erol2025}.

One of the greatest challenges is preserving quantum coherence throughout computation. Quantum states are intrinsically fragile and continuously interact with their surrounding environment, leading to decoherence through energy relaxation and dephasing processes. In addition, imperfections in quantum control, gate operations, state preparation, readout, leakage into unwanted states, and crosstalk between neighbouring qubits introduce errors that accumulate as circuit depth and system size increase~\cite{deLeon2021}. Consequently, increasing the number of physical qubits does not necessarily translate into greater computational capability, since the probability of obtaining reliable results decreases as errors propagate during execution~\cite{Oukaira2025}.

Scalability represents another major challenge. Building useful quantum processors requires not only increasing the number of physical qubits but also maintaining precise calibration, efficient qubit connectivity, reliable control electronics, and stable operating conditions. As quantum devices become larger, wiring complexity, thermal management, fabrication variability, and classical control requirements rapidly increase, making large-scale integration a significant engineering challenge~\cite{deLeon2021,Croot2025}.

Quantum error correction (QEC) provides the theoretical framework for overcoming physical errors and enabling fault-tolerant quantum computation. However, current QEC schemes require substantial resource overheads, as each logical qubit must be encoded into many physical qubits while continuously extracting and decoding error syndromes. As a result, the number of useful logical qubits remains considerably smaller than the number of available physical qubits, limiting the execution of computationally demanding quantum algorithms~\cite{Erol2025,Palsberg2026,Shor1995,KITAEV20032}.

Current quantum algorithms also face important practical limitations. Many proposed quantum advantages assume fault-tolerant hardware, deep quantum circuits, and idealized operating conditions that remain beyond the capabilities of present-day quantum processors. Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs), among the most promising approaches for NISQ devices, remain highly sensitive to noise and optimization difficulties, while state preparation and measurement frequently dominate the overall computational cost~\cite{Wang2026,Raseena2025}.

Finally, software infrastructure, compilation, verification, and benchmarking remain active areas of research. Efficient hardware-aware compilation, reliable validation of quantum computations, and portable software frameworks are still under development. Moreover, the complexity and cost associated with cryogenic systems, specialized fabrication technologies, and control electronics continue to limit the scalability and widespread deployment of QC platforms~\cite{Palsberg2026,Croot2025,Oukaira2025}.

Overall, the current limitations of QC extend beyond hardware performance alone and involve the entire computational stack, from physical implementation to algorithms and software~\cite{paper_dani,nielsen2010quantum}. Continued progress therefore depends on simultaneous advances in qubit coherence, scalable architectures, quantum error correction, algorithm design, software development, and experimental validation before quantum computers can deliver robust and practical computational advantages~\cite{Palsberg2026,Raseena2025}.










\section{Scope and framework of this thesis}

This thesis investigates membrane-active peptides through a multiscale computational framework that connects pathological membrane remodeling, peptide–membrane biophysics, and environment-conditioned peptide design. The broader biological context is provided by the Cancer–Aging–Infection framework and the recurrent alterations in lipid metabolism and membrane organization associated with these conditions. Within this context, the molecular studies focus primarily on $\alpha$-helical antimicrobial peptides and on lipid environments representative of healthy mammalian, bacterial, and cancer-associated membranes.

The computational framework progresses from the molecular characterization of peptide–membrane interactions to reduced models for conformational and sequence optimization. Molecular dynamics and enhanced-sampling simulations are used to examine the structural and thermodynamic basis of membrane recognition, whereas simplified physical Hamiltonians provide a complementary representation for exploring peptide folding and sequence design under defined environmental conditions. These reduced models are subsequently investigated using classical and hybrid quantum–classical optimization strategies, including VQE for interface-dependent conformational optimization and QAOA for environment-conditioned sequence design.

Together, these complementary levels of description provide the conceptual framework of the thesis: molecular simulations characterize how peptide behavior depends on the membrane environment, while reduced physical models translate selected physicochemical principles into tractable optimization problems for peptide modeling and design.
