\section{Hypothesis}
This thesis is based on the main hypothesis that the behavior of cyclic peptides in biologically relevant media is defined by their amino acid sequence, their conformational landscape, and their ability to self-assemble into supramolecular architectures. These features collectively govern how CPs interact with lipid membranes and ultimately define their biological activity.

Because the molecular interactions underlying CP function are highly dynamic and occur at atomic length scales, experimental techniques alone often lack either the spatial resolution or the temporal information required to fully elucidate their mechanisms of action. In this context, MD simulations provide a powerful complementary approach, offering atomistic insight into both structural and dynamical aspects of CP self-assembly and membrane interactions.

A second hypothesis underlying this work is that the rich, high-dimensional data generated by MD simulations---encompassing structural, energetic, and dynamic descriptors---can be systematically exploited to train ML models. When properly encoded, this simulation-derived data can enable ML models to generalize beyond individual simulations and predict CP behavior and activity, thereby accelerating the exploration of sequence space and guiding the design of novel CPs with tailored properties.


\section{General and specific Objectives}
In order to test these hypotheses, the main objective of this doctoral thesis was to deepen the understanding of the relationship between CP sequence and SCPN biological activity, with the aim of predicting their behavior in the presence of biologically relevant lipid membrane models. To achieve this goal, the work was structured around the following specific objectives:

\begin{enumerate}[font=\bfseries]
    \item \textbf{Creation of a CP specific Coarse-Grain (CG) force field.} Previous studies have shown that current CG force fields, like Martini, can reproduce the expected behavior of CPs in presence of healthy mammalian and bacterial membrane models. However, due to their lower resolution, these force fields fail to capture the directionality that characterizes the CP-CP interactions, leading to an overestimation of self-assembly and to the incorrect supramolecular geometries. The first objective of this thesis addressed this issue with the development of MA(R/S)TINI, a chirality-aware adaptation of the general Martini force field capable of reproducing correct CP assembly patterns. Following the release of Martini 3, an updated version of MA(R/S)TINI was introduced, preserving the benefits of the original while refining the CP-CP interaction energy profile to better align with the general force field. This objective constitutes the methodological foundation of the thesis and is addressed in the first and second article of chapter \ref{chap1}.
    \item \textbf{Systematic analysis of sequence–activity relationships in SCPN–membrane interactions.} By enabling microsecond-scale simulations of SCPN–membrane systems, MA(R/S)TINI 3 made it possible to explore cyclic peptide behavior beyond isolated case studies. The second objective of this thesis was to exploit this capability to perform large-scale MD simulations of SCPNs interacting with bacterial membrane models and to analyze the resulting data in a systematic manner. This objective involved the generation and analysis of thousands of MD simulations to identify distinct modes of membrane interaction and to relate them to peptide sequence features. The resulting dataset was further used to train a ML model capable of predicting the behavior of an SCPN in MD simulations using only its sequence as input. This objective is addressed with the fourth publication of this thesis, included as part of chapter \ref{chap2}.
    \item \textbf{Prediction of experimental CP membrane permeability.} The release of CycPeptMPDB\cita{Li2023}---a database containing experimental data of the membrane permeability of CPs---enables the application of ML tools capable of predicting experimental values. The third objective of this thesis was to develop ML models capable of predicting CP membrane permeability from sequence information and to systematically evaluate different representation schemes, data augmentation strategies, and model architectures to maximize predictive performance. This objective is addressed in the fifth publication included in this thesis, which is presented in chapter \ref{chap3}
    \item \textbf{Development of accessible computational tools for the cyclic peptide community.} The final objective of this thesis was to improve the accessibility of advanced MD and ML methodologies for the broader scientific community. To this end, web-based tools were developed to allow non-expert users to generate cyclic peptide and SCPN structures, obtain ready-to-use simulation input files, and access predictive ML models in a straightforward and user-friendly manner. This objective emphasized the democratization of computational techniques and the translation of methodological advances into practical research tools. The first stone to achieve this objective was set with the publication of CYCLOPEpBuilder, presented in the third publication in chapter \ref{chap1}. Subsequent publications progressively extended the platform by incorporating additional functionalities, ultimately enabling the integration of structure generation, simulation workflows, datasets, and ML models.
\end{enumerate}

The designed tasks were expected to provide a deep understanding of the impact of amino acid sequence into the behavior of SCPNs in the presence of bacterial membrane models. Furthermore, to facilitate the access to 3D visualization, MD simulations and accurate ML predictions, all the tools and data generated during this thesis were integrated in CYCLOPEp, a publicly available web server that aims to accelerate CP research.
