\section{Research hypotheses}

This doctoral thesis is built upon two complementary hypotheses.

The first hypothesis is that the interfacial behaviour and membrane selectivity of $\alpha$-helical AMPs arise from the interplay between their intrinsic physicochemical properties and the composition of the surrounding lipid environment. Variations in membrane composition, charge distribution, organization, and polarity are therefore expected to reshape the free-energy landscape governing peptide adsorption and membrane integration, leading to distinct binding preferences and interfacial orientations. These effects can be quantitatively characterized through the combination of unbiased MD and enhanced-sampling simulations by analysing descriptors such as adsorption free energies, insertion depth, tilt and spin angles, and the transverse hydrophobic moment.

The second hypothesis is that peptide folding at heterogeneous interfaces and the design of environment-responsive sequences can be formulated as discrete, physics-based optimization problems. If the relevant interactions between peptide sequence, conformation, and environment are appropriately encoded into a Hamiltonian, simplified models should preserve sufficient physicochemical fidelity to identify low-energy conformations and sequences preferentially stabilized under defined environmental conditions. Binary representations of these Hamiltonians provide a unified formulation that can be explored using exact, heuristic, and hybrid quantum--classical algorithms, enabling their accuracy, computational requirements, and scalability to be systematically evaluated.

\section{General and specific objectives}

The general objective of this doctoral thesis is to develop a multiscale and physically interpretable computational framework for understanding the interaction of $\alpha$-helical membrane-active peptides with lipid bilayers and for designing peptide sequences adapted to specific physicochemical environments. To achieve this goal, the thesis integrates pathophysiological analysis, molecular simulations, free-energy calculations, physics-based modeling, discrete optimization, hybrid quantum--classical algorithms, and computational tool development, while the quantum-computing component is placed within the broader evolution of the field through a quantitative bibliometric analysis.

This general objective is pursued through the following specific objectives:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Establish the pathophysiological basis for membrane-targeted therapeutic strategies within the Cancer--Aging--Infection framework.}

To critically examine the connections among cancer, aging, infection, chronic inflammation, and lipid dysregulation, with particular emphasis on alterations in membrane composition and organization. This analysis seeks to determine whether convergent changes in membrane properties provide a physicochemical basis for selective therapeutic intervention, thereby establishing the biological framework for the subsequent study of membrane-active peptides.

\item \textbf{Characterize the interaction and relative membrane selectivity of representative $\alpha$-helical AMPs.}

To investigate the adsorption, membrane integration, and interfacial orientation of selected $\alpha$-helical AMPs in simplified lipid bilayers representative of healthy mammalian, bacterial, and cancer-cell membranes. Unbiased MD and Well-Tempered Metadynamics (WT-MetaD) are employed to reconstruct the corresponding free-energy landscapes and quantify the influence of membrane composition on adsorption free energies, insertion depth, peptide orientation, and the transverse hydrophobic moment.

\item \textbf{Develop a quantum-compatible model of peptide folding at polar--apolar interfaces.}

To extend discrete tetrahedral lattice models of peptide folding to heterogeneous environments by explicitly representing the transition between polar and apolar media and formulating the corresponding energetic contributions as a quantum-compatible Hamiltonian. Hybrid quantum--classical methods, including the VQE, are evaluated in terms of their ability to recover low-energy conformations, their computational resource requirements, and their scalability relative to classical reference calculations.

\item \textbf{Formulate a physics-based Hamiltonian for environment-conditioned $\alpha$-helical peptide design.}

To develop an interpretable energy function capable of evaluating and optimizing $\alpha$-helical peptide sequences in aqueous, apolar, and membrane-interfacial environments. The Hamiltonian integrates intrinsic helix propensities, local and pairwise residue interactions, electrostatics, solvent polarity, membrane surface charge, and interfacial geometry. Its energetic contributions are normalized relative to random-sequence ensembles, allowing sequences of different lengths and environmental preferences to be compared within a common framework. The resulting optimization problem is investigated using exact enumeration, classical heuristic methods, and hybrid quantum--classical strategies, including the QAOA.

\item \textbf{Translate the environment-conditioned design model into an accessible and reproducible computational platform.}

To implement the sequence analysis and design methodology in \textit{Helix Design Studio}, an open-source platform that enables users to evaluate peptide sequences across different physicochemical environments, inspect their energetic and amphipathic properties, and perform environment-conditioned sequence design. The platform is intended to make the methodology accessible beyond specialist users while maintaining full transparency in the underlying physical model, parameters, and computational workflow.

\end{enumerate}

Together, these objectives define a coherent progression from biological motivation and molecular characterization to physics-based modeling, computational optimization, and practical implementation.
