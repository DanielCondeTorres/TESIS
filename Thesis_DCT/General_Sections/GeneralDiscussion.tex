The studies presented in this thesis address a common question at different levels of molecular resolution: how does the physicochemical environment influence the behavior of membrane-active peptides, and how can this information be incorporated into computational strategies for peptide design? The work began from a broad pathophysiological perspective, examining lipid dysregulation in the context of cancer, aging, and infection, and subsequently moved toward increasingly defined molecular problems: peptide recognition of lipid bilayers, conformational optimization at heterogeneous interfaces, and sequence optimization under prescribed environmental conditions. In parallel, the quantum-computing component was placed within the broader evolution of the field through a quantitative analysis of its scientific growth, intellectual structure, and transition toward hybrid and application-oriented approaches.

This progression also reflects a change in computational scale. Molecular dynamics and enhanced-sampling simulations provide detailed structural and thermodynamic information for a limited number of peptide--membrane systems. Reduced models sacrifice part of this molecular detail but permit broader exploration of conformational or sequence spaces. The central link among these approaches is the explicit consideration of the environment. In the molecular simulations, the environment is represented by the lipid bilayer and its composition; in the folding model, by a polar--apolar interface; and in the sequence-design Hamiltonian, by terms describing solvent polarity, surface charge, electrostatic screening, and interfacial geometry. This common physical dependence provides the basis for integrating the different contributions of the thesis. The bibliometric analysis provides an additional methodological perspective by situating the quantum algorithms explored here within the broader development of contemporary quantum computing.


\section{Lipid remodeling and the pathophysiological context of membrane targeting}

The CAIn framework provided the initial biomedical context for considering lipid membranes as potential sources of therapeutic selectivity \cite{DANI_LIPIDOS} (see Section~\ref{art:lipids_review}). Cancer, aging, and infection are distinct biological processes, but the literature reviewed in this thesis identifies recurrent connections through chronic inflammation, altered lipid metabolism, and changes in membrane organization. These similarities do not define a single membrane phenotype shared by all three conditions. Their relevance lies in showing that lipid homeostasis is repeatedly perturbed in pathological or age-associated states and that these perturbations may modify physicochemical properties involved in molecular recognition.

The nature of the lipid changes differs substantially among the three contexts. In many cancer cells, loss of phospholipid asymmetry and exposure of phosphatidylserine can increase the anionic character of the outer membrane surface. In aging, lipid remodeling is more heterogeneous and depends on tissue, sex, metabolic state, oxidative damage, and cellular phenotype. Infection introduces another level of complexity, since the lipid composition of microbial membranes differs intrinsically from that of mammalian plasma membranes, while infection can also modify lipid metabolism in the host. The CAIn analysis therefore supports lipid dysregulation as a convergent area of interest, without requiring a universal lipid signature.

This distinction is important for the molecular studies performed later in the thesis. The membrane simulations do not attempt to reproduce the entire CAIn landscape. They use simplified lipid bilayers models to isolate physicochemical contrasts relevant to peptide recognition, including differences in surface charge and lipid composition. In this controlled setting, the healthy mammalian-like, bacterial-like, and cancer-associated models provide a tractable basis for asking whether a peptide experiences different free-energy landscapes in different membrane environments.

The broader implication is that membrane selectivity should be considered as an interaction property. A peptide does not possess a fixed degree of selectivity independently of its target. Its behavior depends on the energetic balance established between the sequence, its membrane-bound conformation and orientation, and the molecular properties of the lipid environment. This concept becomes particularly relevant for amphipathic $\alpha$-helical peptides, for which electrostatic attraction and hydrophobic partitioning are strongly coupled to molecular orientation at the interface.



\section{Structural and thermodynamic determinants of AMP–membrane recognition}

The molecular simulation study examined LL-37, magainin-2, pleurocidin, CM15, and clavanin in simplified mammalian-, bacterial-, and cancer-associated membrane models using unbiased MD and WT-MetaD \cite{DANI_METADINAMICA} (see Section~\ref{art:metadynamics}). The simulations showed that membrane association is not adequately described by the peptide--membrane distance alone. Peptide orientation changes substantially during association and insertion, indicating that translational and rotational degrees of freedom contribute jointly to the membrane-bound state.

A recurrent feature of the simulated $\alpha$-helical peptides was the tendency to adopt interfacial orientations in which the helical axis remained approximately parallel to the membrane plane while the hydrophobic face was directed toward the lipid interior. Analysis of the spin angle showed that rotation around the helical axis contributes to this arrangement \cite{DANI_METADINAMICA}. The transverse component of the hydrophobic dipole was consequently associated with favorable interfacial orientations and provided a useful descriptor for interpreting the coupling between amphipathic organization and membrane insertion.

The significance of this result lies less in identifying a single determinant of AMP activity than in showing why scalar descriptors alone are insufficient. Net charge and global hydrophobicity are valuable sequence-level properties, but they do not specify how the residues are spatially arranged once the peptide reaches the membrane. For an amphipathic helix, orientation determines which residues face the aqueous phase, the lipid headgroups, or the hydrophobic core. In the systems examined here, insertion depth, tilt, spin, and hydrophobic-dipole orientation therefore describe different aspects of the same membrane-bound configuration.

Electrostatics contributed strongly to the preference for anionic membranes. The cationic peptides generally interacted more favorably with the bacterial- and cancer-associated models than with the zwitterionic POPC bilayer \cite{DANI_METADINAMICA}. This behavior is consistent with electrostatic recruitment to membranes containing negatively charged lipids, but the magnitude of the preference differed among sequences. Surface charge alone therefore does not account for the complete free-energy landscape. Hydrophobic partitioning, peptide amphipathicity, lipid composition, and orientational adaptation contribute to the final thermodynamic state.

WT-MetaD provided the means to examine these differences quantitatively. Free-energy surfaces were reconstructed as functions of selected collective variables describing peptide position and orientation, and one-dimensional profiles were obtained by thermodynamic projection. The resulting adsorption free energies and transfer free energies provided a quantitative basis for comparing peptide preferences among membrane models \cite{DANI_METADINAMICA}. LL-37 showed the largest differential preference among the peptides examined, although its interaction with the POPC model remained favorable. This result illustrates an important distinction between affinity and selectivity: strong binding to a target membrane does not necessarily imply weak binding to an off-target membrane, and both quantities must be considered when evaluating differential membrane recognition.

The simulations also showed that calculated adsorption energetics can depend strongly on the representation of electrostatics. For some peptide--membrane systems, the use of polarizable coarse-grained water substantially changed the estimated free energies, whereas the effect was considerably smaller for others \cite{DANI_METADINAMICA}. This sensitivity emphasizes that quantitative free-energy values cannot be interpreted independently of the force field and solvent model. It also suggests that highly charged peptide--membrane systems constitute particularly demanding tests for coarse-grained electrostatic representations.

Secondary structure introduced an additional source of model dependence. Martini 2.2 encodes peptide secondary structure through its backbone representation and bonded parameters, which prevents spontaneous interconversion between coil and helical states during standard simulations. The additional magainin-2 simulation performed without the helical restraints produced substantially different membrane-interaction behavior, demonstrating that the assumed folding state affects the resulting peptide--bilayer interaction \cite{DANI_METADINAMICA}. This observation is directly relevant to AMPs because many linear sequences acquire increased helical structure only after encountering a membrane or membrane-mimetic environment.

The coarse-grained simulations therefore resolve peptide orientation, association, and thermodynamic preference efficiently, but they do not provide a complete description of environment-induced folding. This limitation motivated the next stage of the thesis: the use of a reduced conformational model in which the environment itself could enter explicitly into the folding Hamiltonian.

The results from the molecular simulation study support the first research hypothesis of this thesis. Different lipid environments generate distinguishable structural and thermodynamic states for the $\alpha$-helical AMPs considered here, and these differences arise from coupled peptide and membrane properties. The analysis also shows why membrane specificity cannot be reduced to a universal descriptor. The transverse hydrophobic dipole, tilt, spin, insertion depth, electrostatics, and membrane composition are most informative when interpreted collectively.

\section{The evolving quantum-computing landscape and the role of hybrid approaches}

The bibliometric analysis included in this thesis provides a quantitative context for the quantum-computing methodologies explored in the subsequent molecular studies. The analysis covered 31,662 publications indexed in Scopus between 1980 and 2025 and showed a pronounced expansion of the field since approximately 2015, accompanied by increasing thematic diversification and the growth of application-oriented research. 
Co-citation and thematic analyses revealed an intellectual structure in which foundational contributions in quantum information and algorithms remain closely connected to a more recent research core associated with NISQ computing, variational methods, quantum machine learning, and hybrid quantum–classical approaches. VQE and QAOA belong to this broader transition from foundational algorithm development toward problem-specific hybrid computational workflows. 
This context is directly relevant to the quantum component of the present thesis. The peptide-folding and sequence-design studies do not attempt to demonstrate a general quantum advantage. Instead, they investigate whether reduced, physically interpretable biomolecular problems can be formulated in a manner compatible with contemporary quantum and hybrid optimization strategies. The folding study uses VQE to explore low-energy conformational states within a discrete interfacial model, whereas the sequence-design framework employs QAOA as one of several optimization backends for a combinatorial Hamiltonian. In this sense, the two molecular applications examined here represent specific examples of the broader methodological transition identified in the bibliometric analysis.


\section{Environment-dependent peptide folding in a quantum framework}

The quantum-folding study addressed a different representation of the same underlying physical problem. Instead of following the continuous trajectory of an explicitly represented peptide and lipid bilayer, peptide conformation was mapped onto a discrete tetrahedral lattice and evaluated with an effective Hamiltonian \cite{DANI_CUANTICA} (see Section~\ref{art:vqe_cesga}). This reduction makes conformational optimization compatible with a quantum computational workflow, but it also changes the physical interpretation of the result: the calculation searches for favorable configurations within the model and does not reproduce a molecular folding trajectory.

The starting quantum folding framework had been developed for homogeneous environments. Extending it to membrane-active peptides required incorporating the dependence of residue energetics on position relative to a polar--apolar interface. The main methodological development was the introduction of a smooth polynomial representation of this interface. A direct discontinuous classification of residues into two phases would have complicated the quantum encoding and increased resource requirements. The polynomial term allowed the environmental contribution to vary smoothly with position while preserving the existing qubit representation \cite{DANI_CUANTICA}.

This modification was tested with polar, nonpolar, and amphipathic peptide sequences under homogeneous and interfacial conditions. For ten-residue peptides, the model required 22 qubits and generated low-energy conformations consistent with the polarity encoded in the test sequences \cite{DANI_CUANTICA}. The calculation therefore demonstrates that an environmental contribution can be incorporated into a quantum-compatible folding Hamiltonian without adding auxiliary qubits for the interface term.

The result should be interpreted within the resolution of the model. Each amino acid is represented by a reduced interaction site, conformational space is constrained by the tetrahedral lattice, and residue--residue energetics are described through an effective Miyazawa--Jernigan potential. The interface is represented as a static polarity transition and does not contain explicit lipids, water molecules, membrane fluctuations, or specific headgroup interactions. These simplifications are necessary for maintaining the problem within available computational resources and determine the type of structural information that can be extracted.

The scaling of the representation is particularly informative. The number of qubits required by the folding model increases approximately quadratically with peptide length, while the number of Hamiltonian terms increases more steeply \cite{DANI_CUANTICA}. Increasing structural resolution, incorporating side-chain degrees of freedom, or replacing the effective pair potential with more detailed interactions would further increase the computational requirements. The study therefore identifies both the feasibility of the interfacial formulation and the principal limitation of this type of quantum conformational encoding.

No general quantum computational advantage follows from these calculations. Their contribution is methodological: they demonstrate that heterogeneous environmental effects can be incorporated into a quantum folding formulation and provide a concrete basis for assessing qubit requirements and model complexity. This distinction is relevant because it shifts the discussion from the abstract size of quantum Hilbert space to the efficiency of the molecular encoding itself.

The folding study also exposed a useful conceptual separation between conformational prediction and sequence design. Encoding three-dimensional conformational degrees of freedom is expensive because the location and connectivity of each residue must be represented. If the structural scaffold is prescribed, amino-acid identity becomes the principal discrete variable. This observation motivated the subsequent move toward environment-conditioned sequence optimization.


\section{Physics-based environment-conditioned sequence design}


The sequence-design Hamiltonian addresses the dependence of peptide behavior on the environment at a different level of abstraction. The model assumes an $\alpha$-helical scaffold and evaluates candidate sequences through explicit contributions describing intrinsic helical propensity, local sequence context, pairwise residue interactions, solvent polarity, surface charge coupling, and electrostatic screening \cite{DANI_HAMILTONIAN} (see Section~\ref{art:hamiltonian}). The environmental condition is therefore part of the objective function itself.

This feature is central to the design strategy. For membrane-active peptides, a sequence that is favorable in water is not necessarily favorable at a membrane interface, and a peptide that interacts strongly with an anionic membrane may also interact appreciably with neutral or apolar environments. The relevant computational objective can therefore involve differential preference among environments in addition to minimizing an energy score in a single condition.

The Hamiltonian uses a statistical normalization scheme based on random-sequence reference ensembles. Each energetic contribution is placed on a common scale through $Z$-score normalization, which facilitates comparison among terms and among sequences of different lengths \cite{DANI_HAMILTONIAN}. These scores are dimensionless and are meaningful within the defined scoring framework; they should not be interpreted as absolute molecular free energies. Their role is to establish whether a sequence is unusually favorable relative to an appropriate random background under the same model conditions.

Benchmarking against experimentally characterized peptide classes provides an internal test of this formulation. Water-soluble helices and hydrophobic transmembrane model peptides were assigned distinct preferences under aqueous and apolar conditions, while the set of established membrane-active AMPs shifted toward more favorable scores under anionic interfacial conditions compared with non-AMP helical controls \cite{DANI_HAMILTONIAN}. This behavior indicates that the terms included in the Hamiltonian contain sufficient information to reproduce broad environmental preferences of the reference sequences.

The same model also produced structured low-energy sequence ensembles during optimization. In the reported design calculations, optimized sequences formed a separated low-energy basin relative to random decoys and displayed amphipathic patterns consistent with the periodicity of an $\alpha$-helix \cite{DANI_HAMILTONIAN}. These results are relevant because the hydrophobic moment was used primarily as a diagnostic and alignment descriptor, not as an explicit energetic term in the final Hamiltonian. The emergence of amphipathic organization therefore reflects the combined effect of the physical contributions included in the scoring function.

The one-vs-rest calculations provide a more stringent test of environmental selectivity. In these designs, an anionic interface was treated as the target while neutral and cationic interfaces, homogeneous water, and an apolar medium acted as competing environments. The optimized candidates showed that maximizing anionic compatibility alone can also favor other environments, especially water at shallow insertion and apolar media at greater insertion depths. Introducing an explicit specificity term produced sequences with improved separation between the target and competing conditions \cite{DANI_HAMILTONIAN}. This result illustrates why environmental design is fundamentally a multi-condition problem.

The optimization framework was deliberately implemented with several computational backends. Exact enumeration provides a reference solution for tractable binary instances, simulated annealing supplies a classical heuristic for larger problems, and QAOA evaluates the same encoded Hamiltonian within a hybrid quantum--classical workflow \cite{DANI_HAMILTONIAN}. Using a common objective function is particularly valuable because it separates the physical model from the optimization algorithm. Agreement or disagreement among solvers can therefore be examined without changing the underlying peptide energetics.

The QAOA implementation also exposes current scaling constraints. For longer sequences, the number of qubits required to retain a large amino-acid alphabet becomes prohibitive for the available computational setting. The study addressed this limitation by reducing the amino-acid dictionary using clustering in physicochemical property space. An eight-residue alphabet allowed an eight-position design to be represented with 24 qubits, while a four-residue alphabet maintained the same qubit requirement for a twelve-position problem \cite{DANI_HAMILTONIAN}. This strategy preserves selected physicochemical diversity at the cost of restricting sequence space and illustrates the compromises required when adapting biomolecular design problems to present quantum resources.

These results support the second research hypothesis at the level tested in the thesis. Both conformational optimization and sequence design can be expressed through reduced, physically interpretable Hamiltonians and investigated with classical and hybrid quantum--classical algorithms. Their usefulness depends on the fidelity of the reduced model, the quality of the encoding, and the computational resources required as system size increases.





\section{Computational implementation and integration of the framework}


Helix Design Studio provides a practical implementation of the environment-conditioned Hamiltonian and associated design protocols. The platform incorporates single-sequence scoring, penetration-depth scans, single-environment optimization, sequence-family generation, dual-environment comparison, one-vs-rest specificity design, cross-environment optimization, and idealized helical structure export (see Section~\ref{art:web_page}). Its principal contribution is to make the model accessible through a standardized interface while retaining direct access to the individual physical terms and environmental parameters.

The platform also helps clarify the intended interpretation of the model. Scores describe compatibility with a predefined $\alpha$-helical scaffold under a chosen environment. They do not constitute measurements of antimicrobial activity, folding kinetics, membrane permeabilization, or cellular toxicity. These quantities remain separate experimental or higher-resolution computational questions. In practical terms, the platform is most appropriately viewed as a screening and hypothesis-generation tool that can prioritize sequences for subsequent characterization.

The different methods developed throughout the thesis can therefore be arranged according to the type of question and molecular resolution required. MD and WT-MetaD describe selected peptide--membrane systems in considerable structural and thermodynamic detail. The lattice model isolates conformational optimization in a simplified heterogeneous environment. The sequence Hamiltonian removes explicit folding degrees of freedom and permits a much broader search over amino-acid identities. Helix Design Studio exposes the final reduced model in a form suitable for systematic sequence evaluation and design.

This hierarchy also suggests a route for future integration. Candidate sequences identified by the reduced Hamiltonian can be examined using molecular simulation in explicit membrane models, and the corresponding structural and thermodynamic information can be used to assess whether the sequence-level score is associated with the expected membrane-bound behavior. Experimental measurements of binding, secondary structure, membrane perturbation, antimicrobial activity, and host-cell toxicity would provide an independent level of validation and could identify terms of the Hamiltonian that require recalibration.





\section{Limitations and future perspectives}

The bibliometric analysis provides a quantitative view of the evolution and structure of quantum-computing research, but its conclusions are necessarily conditioned by the bibliographic database, search strategy, document coverage, and network metrics employed. The resulting patterns should therefore be interpreted as a structured representation of the retrieved literature rather than as a complete assessment of technological maturity, computational performance, or the overall development of the field.

The interpretation of the molecular simulation results is conditioned by the coarse-grained representation used in the principal peptide--membrane study. Martini 2.2 provides access to long trajectories and efficient exploration of membrane association, but specific atomistic interactions are averaged into effective potentials and secondary structure is normally imposed. Hydrogen-bond networks, side-chain rotamers, cation--$\pi$ geometries, and spontaneous folding transitions are therefore not represented with atomistic detail. The magainin-2 comparison confirms that this structural constraint can affect the peptide--membrane interaction and should be considered when extending the conclusions to conformationally flexible AMPs.

Free-energy reconstruction introduces a second level of approximation. Metadynamics depends on the selection of collective variables, and slow degrees of freedom not represented in the chosen CV space can impair convergence or introduce hysteresis. Local lipid rearrangement, peptide aggregation, and other collective processes may remain only partially sampled. The calculated PMFs also describe single-peptide association. AMP-mediated permeabilization and pore formation frequently involve concentration-dependent and cooperative phenomena that fall outside the thermodynamic process sampled in these simulations.

The quantum folding model is limited principally by its spatial discretization and effective interaction potential. Its low-energy states belong to the tetrahedral lattice representation and cannot be interpreted as atomistically resolved peptide structures. The polynomial interface improves the treatment of environmental heterogeneity within this representation but remains an implicit and static approximation to a fluctuating lipid--water boundary. Increasing conformational detail rapidly increases qubit requirements and Hamiltonian complexity.

The sequence-design model makes a different approximation by fixing the peptide on an idealized $\alpha$-helical scaffold. This is appropriate for studying environment-conditioned sequence preferences within that structural family, but it excludes sequences whose preferred states contain kinks, partial unfolding, $\beta$-structure, or other conformations. Environmental effects are encoded through effective terms and do not reproduce the molecular heterogeneity of an explicit membrane. The reduction of the amino-acid alphabet used for larger quantum-compatible instances introduces an additional restriction on the accessible design space.

Current quantum resources impose further limitations on both applications. Noise, finite coherence, circuit depth, shot requirements, variational optimization, and qubit count constrain the size of tractable calculations. Classical methods remain indispensable as reference solvers and, for many of the problem sizes considered here, provide highly competitive or exact solutions. The thesis therefore establishes quantum-compatible biomolecular formulations and evaluates their feasibility; it does not establish a practical quantum speedup over state-of-the-art classical optimization.

Within these limits, a consistent result emerges across the different computational levels: the environment can be incorporated explicitly into physically interpretable models of peptide behavior. At high molecular resolution, lipid composition modifies adsorption and interfacial organization. In the reduced folding model, polarity changes the energetic preference of conformational states. In sequence design, solvent and membrane properties alter the ranking of candidate amino-acid sequences. This continuity supports the use of environment-conditioned modeling as a useful principle for membrane-active peptide research.

The next stage will require stronger coupling between these levels of description. Prospective sequences generated by the Hamiltonian should be tested through independent molecular simulations and experimental measurements, while information from those studies can be used to refine the reduced energetic terms. Improvements in peptide and membrane representations may permit greater conformational flexibility to be included in the design objective. In parallel, future developments in quantum hardware and algorithms will determine whether larger and more detailed Hamiltonians can be treated competitively.

The main contribution of the thesis is therefore a multiscale computational framework in which membrane biophysics, conformational optimization, and sequence design are connected through explicit environmental variables. Each level answers a different question and carries its own approximations. Their combination provides a physically interpretable route for investigating membrane specificity and for generating peptide candidates whose predicted properties can be tested at progressively higher levels of molecular and experimental resolution.
