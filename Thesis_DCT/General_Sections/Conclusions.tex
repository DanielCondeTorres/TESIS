\chapter{General conclusions}\label{conclusions}
\chapterquote{We don't get to choose how we start in this life. Real 'greatness' is what you do with the hand you're dealt.}{Victor Sullivan, Uncharted 3}

\newpage
This doctoral thesis has developed a multiscale computational framework for investigating how the physicochemical environment influences the interaction, conformational preferences, and design of $\alpha$-helical membrane-active peptides. The work integrates pathophysiological analysis, molecular dynamics, enhanced sampling, reduced physical models, discrete optimization, and hybrid quantum--classical algorithms. Across these levels of representation, environmental dependence emerges as a common element connecting peptide--membrane recognition, conformational optimization, and sequence design.

The analysis of the Cancer--Aging--Infection framework established the biomedical context for considering membrane composition and organization as relevant variables in molecular recognition. Cancer, aging, and infection are associated with recurrent changes in inflammatory signaling, lipid metabolism, and membrane organization, with specific manifestations that depend on the biological system considered \cite{DANI_LIPIDOS} (see Section~\ref{art:lipids_review}). These observations support the relevance of lipid remodeling within the CAIn framework and provide a pathophysiological basis for investigating how changes in membrane physicochemical properties can contribute to differential recognition by membrane-active peptides.

At the molecular level, unbiased MD and WT-MetaD simulations of magainin-2, pleurocidin, CM15, LL-37, and clavanin showed that their interaction with lipid bilayers depends on both peptide properties and membrane composition \cite{DANI_METADINAMICA} (see Section~\ref{art:metadynamics}). Association with the membrane involved coupled changes in insertion depth, tilt, rotation around the helical axis, and orientation of the amphipathic surface. Within the systems examined, the transverse component of the hydrophobic dipole emerged as a particularly informative descriptor of interfacial organization, with favorable membrane-bound configurations orienting the hydrophobic face of the helix toward the lipid interior. Electrostatic interactions promoted association with anionic environments, while sequence-dependent differences in amphipathicity, hydrophobic partitioning, and molecular orientation modulated the resulting membrane preference. LL-37 displayed the largest differential preference among the peptides considered, illustrating how thermodynamic characterization can distinguish peptide-specific responses to membrane composition.

Enhanced sampling provided the quantitative thermodynamic basis for these observations. Free-energy surfaces constructed from peptide position and orientation enabled comparison of adsorption and transfer energetics among the different peptide--membrane systems \cite{DANI_METADINAMICA}. The calculations showed that membrane recognition is described by a multidimensional free-energy landscape in which translational and rotational degrees of freedom are coupled. The dependence of the calculated energetics on the electrostatic representation further highlighted the importance of solvent polarization in highly charged peptide--membrane systems. In parallel, the comparison of different structural representations of magainin-2 confirmed that peptide secondary structure has a substantial influence on membrane association. Together, these results support the first research hypothesis by showing that membrane composition and peptide physicochemical properties jointly determine distinct interfacial and thermodynamic states.

The conformational component of the thesis extended the treatment of environmental dependence to a reduced quantum-compatible model of peptide folding \cite{DANI_CUANTICA} (see Section~\ref{art:vqe_cesga}). A tetrahedral-lattice Hamiltonian was adapted to heterogeneous polar--apolar environments through a smooth polynomial representation of the interface, allowing residue--environment interactions to vary according to their position relative to the two phases. The formulation was implemented within a VQE-based hybrid quantum--classical workflow and evaluated using polar, nonpolar, and amphipathic ten-residue peptides. The resulting 22-qubit representation recovered low-energy conformations consistent with the environmental preferences encoded in the model, while the interfacial contribution was incorporated without additional qubits relative to the corresponding homogeneous formulation. This work establishes a quantum-compatible representation of interface-dependent conformational optimization and provides a quantitative description of the computational resources associated with the encoding.

The environment-conditioned sequence-design Hamiltonian developed in the subsequent stage transferred the same physical principle from conformational space to sequence space \cite{DANI_HAMILTONIAN} (see Section~\ref{art:hamiltonian}). The model combines intrinsic helical propensities, local sequence effects, pairwise residue interactions, solvent polarity, membrane surface charge, and electrostatic screening within a common energy function. Normalization against random-sequence ensembles places these contributions on a comparable statistical scale and enables the energetic compatibility of peptide sequences to be evaluated across aqueous, apolar, and interfacial environments. Benchmarking with experimentally characterized peptide classes reproduced broad environmental preferences: water-soluble helices were favored in polar conditions, transmembrane model peptides showed strong compatibility with apolar environments, and established membrane-active AMPs displayed more favorable scores under anionic interfacial conditions. Sequence optimization also generated low-energy ensembles with amphipathic organization and hydrophobic periodicities characteristic of $\alpha$-helical geometry.

The same Hamiltonian supported different formulations of peptide optimization. Single-environment design identified sequences favored under a prescribed condition, while cross-environment and one-vs-rest objectives incorporated differential environmental preference directly into the optimization criterion. In the latter case, candidate sequences were optimized for compatibility with an anionic interface while simultaneously accounting for neutral and cationic interfaces, homogeneous water, and apolar conditions \cite{DANI_HAMILTONIAN}. Exact enumeration, simulated annealing, and QAOA were applied to the same binary-encoded physical objective, providing classical and hybrid quantum--classical strategies for exploring the sequence landscape. The quantum-compatible calculations additionally quantified how peptide length and amino-acid alphabet size affect encoding requirements. These results support the second research hypothesis by demonstrating that both interface-dependent folding and environment-conditioned sequence design can be expressed as discrete physical Hamiltonians and investigated with multiple computational optimization strategies.

Helix Design Studio translates the sequence-design framework into an open-source computational platform for peptide scoring, environmental comparison, penetration analysis, sequence optimization, family generation, and multi-environment design (see Section~\ref{art:web_page}). The platform retains access to the individual energetic contributions and environmental parameters of the Hamiltonian, providing a transparent and reproducible implementation of the model. In this way, the environment-conditioned design methodology developed in this thesis is made available through a unified computational workflow for peptide analysis and candidate generation.

Overall, the work presented in this thesis connects the biological motivation provided by lipid remodeling with the molecular characterization of peptide--membrane interactions, environment-dependent conformational optimization, and sequence design. Across these different levels of resolution, the physicochemical environment remains an explicit component of the computational description. This common principle provides continuity between molecular simulation, reduced physical Hamiltonians, classical optimization, and hybrid quantum--classical approaches, establishing a coherent framework for the computational study and environment-conditioned design of $\alpha$-helical membrane-active peptides.
